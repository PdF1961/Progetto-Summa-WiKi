# Étienne Gilson (1884–1978)

French historian of medieval philosophy, *chargé de cours* at the Sorbonne and director of studies at the École Pratique des Hautes Études when he wrote [[le-thomisme-gilson]] (1922 2nd ed.). One of the major figures of the 20th-century Thomistic revival alongside Garrigou-Lagrange and Maritain, credited (with Maritain) as one of the two authorities Mons. Antonio Livi cites in the preface to [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] as guiding his own philosophical formation.

## Core historiographical stance (per [[le-thomisme-gilson]] preface)

Argued that mainstream histories of philosophy contained an unjustified gap — treating the period from Plotinus to Bacon/Descartes as near-total sterility — and that 13th-century thinkers (Aquinas, Duns Scotus above all) belong among history's genuinely great philosophers, not merely theologians dressed in Aristotelian syllogisms.

## Central thesis on Aquinas: "the Thomistic problem"

Frames Aquinas's entire philosophical project as the solution to a specific historical conflict: 13th-century Latin Christendom, newly in possession of the full Aristotelian corpus (via Toledo translators, Avicenna, Averroes) split into the **Latin Averroists** (Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia — full acceptance of Aristotle/Averroes, including eternity of the world, a single intellect for all humanity, and denial of personal immortality) and the **Augustinian-Franciscans** (Bonaventure — rejecting Aristotle for Platonic exemplarism, illumination, plurality of substantial forms). Aquinas's task, in Gilson's famous formula, was to **"christianize Aristotle"**: reintroduce exemplarism and creation, preserve providence, reconcile the unity of substantial form with the soul's immortality — walking "the narrow ridge" (*l'arête étroite*) between the two camps, using what was true in Aristotle against the Augustinians while using Christian truths absent from pure Peripateticism against the Averroists.

## The unity of the system ("L'esprit du thomisme," concluding chapter)

Argues the entire fabric of Thomism is woven from a small number of principles, ultimately different aspects of a single idea: **being** (*l'idée d'être*). Being, unity, truth, and goodness are convertible: a thing's truth is grounded in its being (agreement between intellect and the thing's essence), and its goodness in its being desirable in proportion to its degree of perfection. The universe is a **hierarchy of participation** in divine being — angels *prope Deus*, material forms *prope nihil*, man as the horizon between spirit and body — ordered by the principle that "the more or less can only be measured against a maximum, the relative only against the absolute" (echoing *S.Th.* I, 44, 1).

Crucially, Gilson **rejects reading this hierarchical, participatory cosmos as Neo-Platonic/Plotinian**: while the Pseudo-Dionysius supplies the *framework* of hierarchy, its *content* is not Plotinus's. Aquinas's God is Augustine's personal, freely-choosing Creator-God (Jehovah), not Plotinus's impersonal One — "between the freely created universe and the creating God there is an unbridgeable abyss, and no continuity but that of order: properly speaking, the world is an **ordered discontinuity**." Likewise Aquinas's account of soul, body, and knowledge is Aristotelian (the soul's near-total dependence on sense knowledge, no direct intuition of the intelligible), not Platonic (which permits reminiscence and ecstatic reunion with the One).
