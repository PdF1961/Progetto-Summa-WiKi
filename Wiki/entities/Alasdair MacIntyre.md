# Alasdair MacIntyre (b. 1929)

Scottish-born moral philosopher, best known for *After Virtue* (1981; *Dopo la virtù*), one of the most influential works of 20th-century moral philosophy. Emigrated to the United States; taught at Notre Dame among other institutions.

## Intellectual trajectory (per his own 2006 preface to the 2nd Italian edition of *Dopo la virtù*)

- Earlier works (*A Short History of Ethics* 1966, *Secularisation and Moral Change* 1967, *Against the Self-Images of the Age* 1971) already argued that moral philosophy must draw on history and anthropology, against the "Oxford armchair" style of conceptual analysis.
- At the time of writing *After Virtue* (1981) he was already an **Aristotelian but not yet a Thomist** — his treatment of Aquinas at the end of chapter 13 of that book reflects this.
- Became a **Thomist afterward**, for three converging reasons he identifies explicitly: (1) realized his account of the good in terms of practices, traditions, and narrative unity of human lives needed explicit grounding in **metaphysics** (found in *Summa Theologiae* Ia q.5, on the good) rather than resting on an implicit, undefended assumption; (2) needed a **biological** basis for attributing virtues/vices to humans, not necessarily Aristotelian, which he develops via the notion of human dependence on animality; (3) adopted Aquinas's doctrine of **misericordia** (mercy) — the "virtues of acknowledged dependence" — as a point where Aquinas decisively goes beyond Aristotle.
- Developed the 1981 theses further in *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988), *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (1990), and *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999).
- Explicitly denies being a "communitarian" in the liberal/communitarian debate sense, and denies nostalgia for the past; his critique targets both liberalism and its mirror-image, contemporary economic conservatism, as equally corrosive of the community needed to pursue a shared human good.
- Defends his position against charges of relativism via the concept of **traditions of rational enquiry** (see [[Unità Narrativa della Vita e Tradizione]]): rival traditions (Aristotelian-Thomism, Madhyamaka Buddhism, Anglo-American utilitarianism) can be judged from within by whether they can resolve their own unsolved problems — including, crucially, by the rival tradition's own criteria — without requiring a neutral, tradition-independent standard.
