## Question 1 — Il Male (read in full)

Foundational metaphysics of evil as privation — see the dedicated concept page [[Il Male come Privazione]] for full detail: evil has no positive being (art. 1); exists only in the good, as privation of a due perfection (art. 2); the good is only the accidental cause of evil (art. 3); evil divides into *pena* and *colpa* (art. 4); there is more evil in *colpa* than in *pena* (art. 5).

## Question 2 — I Peccati (read in full)

Twelve articles analyzing the structure of sin: an act is present in every sin, even sins of omission, when considered from the standpoint of cause even if not from the act itself (art. 1); sin consists of privation, interior act, and exterior act together, not the will's act alone (art. 2-3); **no human act is indifferent in relation to reason** — every act is good or bad relative to reason, even if indifferent considered in itself apart from reason (art. 4); no act is indifferent in relation to its **circumstances** either, since no act occurs without circumstances, and the fundamental circumstance is the end (art. 5); a circumstance can constitute a new species of sin (e.g., theft becomes sacrilege if committed in a sacred place) or merely aggravate an existing one, potentially to the point of turning a venial sin mortal (art. 6-8); **against the Stoics**, sins are not all equal — because unlike "pure" privation (death), sin as an "impure" privation (like sickness short of death) admits degrees; graver sin opposes a greater good, with sins against God graver than against neighbor, and among the latter, murder (life in act) graver than lust-sins (life in potency) graver than theft (art. 9-10); sin diminishes but does not entirely destroy the "natural good" (the soul's disposition toward grace) — argued via the physical analogy of a disposition diminished by subtraction (can go to zero) versus by addition of a contrary (may or may not destroy the underlying subject) (art. 11-12).

## Question 3 — La Causa del Peccato (read in full)

Central concern: **can God or the devil be, in any sense, causes of sin?** God cannot be cause of sin (art. 1) — physically, because sin-as-failure requires a deficient agent-principle, excluded in infinite power; morally, because God's will is its own end and cannot fail to attain it, nor can God draw creatures away from himself while being the one who draws all things toward himself as the Supreme Good. Yet **God does cause the physical act underlying a sin** (as first mover of every act of free will, generically as being, specifically as movement) **while the moral deformity is caused by man alone** — analogized to how a plant's imperfect fruit traces to the plant's own defective generative power, not to the perfect motion of the first heaven that set it growing; or how walking's power comes from the motive faculty but limping from a defect in the shin (art. 2). The devil is not, properly speaking, a cause of sin either — only something that "counsels or disposes" is, since neither necessitates an effect; the will is moved by its object only through persuasion, never by necessity except toward beatitude/God-as-known-in-se (which humans do not experience directly in this life), so no earthly good, however attractive, moves the will of necessity (art. 3). The devil *can* incite sin by internally persuading — not by illuminating the intellect (which cannot be deceived once truly illuminated) but by stirring sensory/imaginative representations via the body's "animal spirits" and humors, exploiting susceptibility to passion (art. 4) — but **not all sins are instigated by the devil**; many follow directly from free will's own failure to restrain disordered appetites, and men would sin even if the devil did not exist (art. 5). Extensive treatment of **ignorance as a cause of sin** in various modes (universal vs. particular moral knowledge, the famous "archer who thinks the man behind the bush is a deer" case distinguishing homicide from parricide by degree of ignorance) (art. 6-8); sin committed **through weakness/fragilità** (passion overwhelming reason, against Socrates's "no one knowingly does wrong") — passion can diminish or even suppress voluntariness depending on whether it precedes or follows the will's act (art. 9-11); sin committed **through malice** (an interior habit rather than external passion) is graver than sin through weakness, for three reasons (more the agent's own principle, habitual rather than episodic, and reflecting a will *habitually* turned to evil rather than occasionally overcome) (art. 12-13); and the **sin against the Holy Spirit** as final impenitence — in the strict sense (despair, presumption, obstinacy) unforgivable per se, though not absolutely impossible to forgive given the power of grace and the unconfirmed state of free will in this life (art. 14-15).

## Question 4 — Il Peccato Originale (read in full)

Against the Pelagians (who denied original sin's transmission, voiding the point of redemption): humanity is considered both as individuals and as a single collective "body" under Adam as principle; original justice was given to Adam both personally and as principle of human nature, so its loss is likewise transmitted as principle, not as personal fault — original sin has no character of personal guilt for the individual as such (it is not voluntary for the individual), but has the character of guilt for the individual *as a member of the human nature-as-one-man*, exactly as a murderer's hand, blameless in itself, shares the guilt of the whole person's will (art. 1). Original sin's material element is **disordered concupiscence** (loss of reason's rule over the lower appetites); its formal element is the **loss of original justice** (art. 2). It resides in the rational soul as its proper subject, transmitted through the semen as instrumental cause (art. 3) — first in the soul's essence, then in its powers (following the order of generation, where substance precedes accidents) (art. 4) — and first in the **will** specifically, since virtue/vice properly belong to the rational part and original sin is a *pena*, and our acts merit punishment only insofar as voluntary (art. 5). It is transmitted to all who descend from Adam **seminally** (art. 6) — even to a hypothetical person formed newly from earth-matter but "moved" only materially via Adam's line would not contract it (art. 7) — and, crucially, **the sins of one's proximate parents are NOT similarly transmitted**, because original justice (unlike acquired personal grace) pervaded all the way to the body and so was transmissible seminally, while personal grace/actual sins pertain to the soul alone and do not reach the body (art. 8).

## Question 5 — La Pena del Peccato Originale (read in full)

The privation of the vision of God is the fitting punishment for original sin (art. 1) — but **not the pena of sense** (positive suffering), which is due only to actual/personal sin, for three reasons: natural-order defects trace to personal vice, supra-natural defects (like loss of the beatific vision) trace to a defect of nature itself; the proportion of punishment to fault (privation of vision ~ turning away from God; sense-pain ~ turning toward creatures) only obtains the second component where there was an actual "conversio," absent in original sin alone; and sense-pain is due to one who actually does evil, not merely one disposed toward it (art. 2). Extensive, careful discussion of whether **children who die with only original sin (unbaptized) suffer interior sorrow** over their loss — Aquinas's considered answer: no, because their natural (not supernatural/faith-based) knowledge tells them man is made for beatitude but not that this specifically consists in the vision of God, so they do not grieve a loss they do not know they've suffered — an ordered application of the general principle that grieving disproportionately for what one could never naturally have attained (like a peasant grieving for not seizing a kingdom) is itself disordered (art. 3). Death and life's other evils are punishments consequent upon original sin (not the punishment itself, but its consequence, like blindness being a judge's sentence and consequent begging being what follows) — against Origen's pre-existence-of-souls account of differential suffering, defended instead via parental merit/demerit affecting bodily-transmitted consequences (art. 4). **Death is against man's nature considered according to form (the soul) though not according to matter (the body)** — the body, composed of contrary elements as required to serve the soul's sensory/intellectual ends, is thereby naturally corruptible, but God, omnipotent, prevented this material necessity from actualizing in the first man before the Fall; immortality is therefore natural to man *qua form* even though corruptibility is natural to him *qua matter*, reconciled originally only by a supernatural gift (art. 5).

## Question 6 — La Scelta Umana (single article, read in full)

Free will as a doctrine of proportion between intellect/will and form/inclination in non-rational nature, but crucially *not* reducible to it: unlike natural inclination (individualized by matter, determined to one object) or animal appetite (individual sensory form, but object-flexible in the moment), the will's object (the good known universally by intellect) is *itself* universal, so inclination is not determined to any single object. Distinguishes the *exercise* of the will's act (which is moved by an external agent — ultimately God, since no other incorporeal mover is available, and an infinite regress of prior willed "counsels" is impossible) from the *specification* of the act by its object (the will is necessitated only by a good perfectly convenient in every respect — beatitude itself, not known as such in this life — while any particular good, however attractive, can be foregone in favor of another). Conclusion: the will is not necessitated in its exercise, and is necessitated by its object only in the very limited case of naturally-necessary goods (like existing); with respect to habits or passions (which can be moderated, e.g. calming one's own anger), the will remains free.
