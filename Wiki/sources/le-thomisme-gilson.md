# Source: Le Thomisme: Introduction au système de Saint Thomas d'Aquin

**Author**: Étienne Gilson
**Format**: OCR'd PDF (French, 1922 2nd ed. scan, heavily garbled OCR in places), ~99,000 words

## Reading note

Read in full: **Chapter I** ("Le problème thomiste" — biography/works catalog plus "Saint Thomas et l'aristotélisme," the framing of Aquinas's historical task) and **Chapter XV** ("L'esprit du thomisme," the concluding synthesis). The 13 intervening chapters, covering the detailed exposition of the system (proofs of God's existence, divine attributes, creation, angels, soul-body union, intellect, will, human act, virtues, final end), are recorded below by verified table of contents only.

## Author's own framing (Préface)

Gilson explicitly protests a "singular gap" in how the history of philosophy is taught: ancient philosophy and modern philosophy (Descartes onward) receive sustained attention, while the stretch from Plotinus to Bacon/Descartes is treated as near-total sterility, and even genuinely great 13th-century philosophers (Aquinas, Duns Scotus — Gilson names them as belonging, without qualification, "to the race of thinkers truly worthy of the name... great philosophers for all time") are dismissed as mere theologians dressed in Aristotelian syllogisms. The book (based on his 1913-14 Lille lectures) is explicitly framed as an **introduction**, not an exhaustive exposition — meant to let a reader with no prior idea of Aquinas's system grasp its "guiding lines and general structure."

## Chapter I — "The Thomistic problem"

The historical-philosophical situation Aquinas inherited: 13th-century Latin Christendom gains full access to Aristotle's *Physics* and *Metaphysics*, Avicenna's abridgment, and Averroes's commentaries (via the Toledo translators), confronting a complete, systematized pagan philosophy far superior to the fragmentary 12th-century syntheses. Three positions crystallize:

1. **Latin Averroists** (Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia): accept Aristotle-via-Averroes wholesale, including the eternity of the world, a single intellect for the whole human species, and consequently no personal immortality, no providence, no reward/punishment after death.
2. **Augustinian-Franciscans** (Bonaventure, per Gilson the clearest witness): diagnose Aristotle's fundamental error as rejecting the Platonic doctrine of Ideas — hence God (on this reading of Aristotle) knows only himself, exercises no providence, and the world's order collapses into either chance or fatal astral determinism. Bonaventure defends Platonic exemplarism, Augustinian illumination (against Aristotelian abstraction), and plurality of substantial forms (against the single-substantial-form thesis threatening the soul's immortality).
3. **Aquinas's "narrow ridge"**: Aristotle's metaphysics has real lacunae (creation, immortality of the soul left unresolved) but his physics is incomparably superior to any available alternative — its triumph was foreseeable, so the urgent task was to **"christianize Aristotle"**: reintroduce exemplarism and creation, preserve providence, reconcile unity of substantial form with the soul's immortality, show that Aristotelian physics, rightly completed, *supports* rather than threatens Christian truth. Aquinas pursued this "attacked by the Franciscan John Peckham (1270), declared suspect by his own Order's general chapter (1282), still caught up in Bishop Étienne Tempier's 1277 condemnation of 219 Averroist/Peripatetic articles" — pressed from both sides, maintaining against the Augustinians what he held true in Aristotle, and against the Aristotelians the Christian truths Peripateticism had missed.
- Gilson's closing observation: after 600 years and countless attempts to build apologetics on new foundations, the Church still lives by Aquinas's thought and wants to continue doing so — itself a sign, in Gilson's view, of the intellectual suppleness and logical rigor with which the system was built.
- Includes a detailed **works catalog** (following Mandonnet's and Grabmann's chronologies) of philosophical commentaries, theological/philosophical/political treatises, philosophical opuscula, and disputed/quodlibetal questions.

## Chapter XV — "The spirit of Thomism"

- **Being as root of the transcendentals**: a thing's truth is grounded in its being (agreement of intellect with the thing's essence, itself grounded in agreement with the eternal divine thought); its goodness in being desirable in proportion to its degree of perfection. Being, unity, truth, goodness are convertible, all aspects of the one idea of being.
- **The universe as hierarchy of participation**, ordered by the principle that degree can only be measured relative to a maximum/absolute; between God (pure being) and sheer nothingness sit angels (*prope Deus*) and material forms (*prope nihil*), with man as the frontier between spirit and body.
- **Analogy, not univocity nor equivocity**, is what makes this hierarchy possible without collapsing Creator into creature: creaturely being is only an image/imitation of divine being, owing it everything, adding nothing to it, subtracting nothing from it.
- **Explicit rejection of the "Aquinas as Neo-Platonist" reading**: the Pseudo-Dionysius supplies only the hierarchical *framework*, not its content or ordering principle. Aquinas's God is Augustine's personal, freely-creating God (Jehovah), not Plotinus's impersonally-emanating One — hence "the world is an **ordered discontinuity**" (*une discontinuité ordonnée*), the "antipodes" of Neo-Platonism. The same divergence holds for the soul/body/knowledge doctrine: Aristotelian (sense-dependent, no direct intuition of the intelligible), not Platonic (reminiscence, ecstatic reunion with the One).
