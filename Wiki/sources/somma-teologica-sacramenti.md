## Structure, confirmed from each question's own prologue

Q.60's prologue lays out the whole sacramental treatise's plan explicitly: sacraments in general first (what a sacrament is, q.60; their necessity, q.61; effects, q.62-63; cause, q.64; number, q.65), then each sacrament individually — Baptism, Confirmation (q.72), Eucharist (q.73), Penance (q.84), Extreme Unction, Holy Orders, and Matrimony (the last three completed only in the posthumous *Supplementum*, per [[somma-teologica-struttura]]'s earlier finding). Within Baptism, q.66's prologue further divides: the sacrament itself, then its preparatory rites — circumcision (q.70, its historical Old Testament precursor) and catechism/exorcism (q.71, its immediate liturgical preparation). Within the Eucharist, q.73's prologue gives the same kind of explicit seven-part roadmap: the sacrament in itself (q.73) → its matter (q.74) → transubstantiation (q.75) → the mode of Christ's presence (q.76) → the accidents that remain (q.77) → its form (q.78) → its effects (q.79) → recipients (q.80) → Christ's own institution of it (q.81) → its minister (q.82) → its rite (q.83).

## The sacrament as sign (q.60 a.1)

The opening article defends the definition of sacrament as belonging to the genus of *signs*, against an etymological objection (*sacramentum* from *sacrare*, "to consecrate," suggesting cause rather than sign) and a scriptural objection (Tobit/Ephesians use "sacramentum" to mean something *hidden*, which seems opposed to signification). Aquinas's resolution (not fully re-transcribed here, flagged for a future closer read) reconciles both: a sacrament is a sign of a sacred reality *insofar as it sanctifies* — sign and cause are not opposed in this case, which becomes the load-bearing premise for q.62's doctrine that sacraments *cause* grace precisely *by signifying* it.

## Sacraments as instrumental causes of grace (q.62)

Q.62's Latin prooemium (this remains a parallel Latin-Italian edition, as already noted for the Trinity treatise in [[somma-teologica-trinita]]) states the question's scope directly: *"utrum sacramenta novae legis sint causa gratiae... utrum talis virtus in sacramentis derivetur a passione Christi"* — do the sacraments of the New Law cause grace, and if so, does that causal power derive from Christ's Passion? Objection 2 of a.1 sharpens the contrast with the Old Law's sacraments, which Paul calls "elementi poveri e deboli" (Gal 4:9) because — on the objector's reading — *"non contenevano né causavano la grazia"*, unlike the Christian sacraments, which *"contengono e causano la grazia."* This is the primary-text location of the instrumental-causality doctrine already summarized (via secondary synthesis) in [[I Sacramenti nella Sintesi Tomistica]].

## Transubstantiation (q.75 aa.1-4)

The most substantively verified doctrine in this batch. Article 3 ("Se la sostanza del pane... venga annichilata, o si risolva nella materia preesistente") poses the two rival explanations Aquinas rejects: that consecration **annihilates** the bread's substance outright, or that it **resolves** it back into pre-existing prime matter (by analogy with ordinary substantial change, e.g. air becoming fire, where the prior form persists potentially in matter). Both are refuted. Objection 2 states the rival "resolves into pre-existing matter" theory precisely, quoting Ambrose (*De myst.* 54: *"Prima della consacrazione si designa un'altra sostanza, dopo la consacrazione si indica il corpo di Cristo"*) in its own support — but Aquinas's *in contrario* (Augustine: *"Dio non è la causa della tendenza al non essere"* — God is not the cause of a tendency toward non-being) sets up his actual doctrine: neither annihilation nor resolution into matter, but a **direct conversion of the whole substance of bread into the whole substance of Christ's body** (*transubstantiation* proper — a change unlike any natural substantial change, since it converts substance-to-substance directly with no shared underlying matter, which is why the *accidents* of bread and wine must remain without their proper subject, the topic of q.77). This is the primary-text confirmation of the doctrine [[I Sacramenti nella Sintesi Tomistica]] already summarizes via Garrigou-Lagrange.

## What remains unread

qq.61, 63-65 (necessity, sacramental character, causal mechanism, the number seven), all of Baptism's particulars (qq.66-71 — ministers, recipients including the infant-baptism articles, circumcision, catechesis), Confirmation (q.72), the Eucharist's matter/form articles in full (qq.74, 76 aa.2-8, 78), its effects in full (q.79, beyond the opening article), and qq.80-83 (recipients, Christ's own institution at the Last Supper, the minister, and the rite/Mass) are confirmed structurally via their own prologues (quoted/paraphrased above) but not read article-by-article. Given the vault's existing Christological material in [[Incarnazione, Unione Ipostatica e Mariologia]], q.76 (mode of Christ's real presence — bearing directly on the hypostatic union material already ingested) is the highest-priority remaining target.
