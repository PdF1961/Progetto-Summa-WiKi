## Chapter 9 — Aquinas's metaphysics of essence and existence, and the five ways

- Aquinas's early *De Ente et Essentia* distinguishes two senses of *ens* (being): the "is" of the existential quantifier, and the sense in which asserting something *is* asserts it is *actual*. Every substance we perceive is a composite of form and matter (*essentia*); that a substance's essence is realized in existence at all is a further fact — its **esse** (act of existing) is other than its *essentia*. We can know *what* a phoenix is without knowing *whether* it exists. This essence/existence composition presupposes a source of existence whose *essentia just is* his *esse* — God.
- The *Summa contra Gentiles* (begun before 1260, written in Italy) is directed at "gentiles" — pagan thinkers who reasoned to truths about God's nature without revelation. Aquinas's project: philosophical enquiry, rightly conducted, arrives at conclusions congruent with and pointing toward revealed truth; getting the natural order wrong produces defective knowledge of God.
- Aquinas's Aristotelianism was almost unqualified, but not quite: on the eternity of the world, he held **neither** Aristotle's arguments for it **nor** the arguments against it are conclusive — it's undecidable by natural reason, so revelation-based creation doctrine contradicts no defensible philosophical thesis. On happiness: genuine but always **imperfect** happiness is achievable in this life; no finite object/state of affairs could satisfy the natural human desire for perfected happiness — only God's self-revelation (grasped through faith, hope, charity) can supply the object of that further desire.
- **The five ways, third way, developed at length**: contingent beings depend on other causal agencies for their existence and operation. An infinite chain of purely dependent beings would itself have the character of a dependent being and so would still require an external explanation for its existence — therefore something not itself dependent (existing necessarily, self-determining, requiring no external cause) must exist. "This everyone speaks of as God" (*S.Th.* Ia, 2.3). MacIntyre stresses the argument does **not** show this God created a temporally-finite world — only that the universe depends on God at every moment for its continued existence, whether or not it had a temporal beginning.
- **On God's existence vs. finite existence**: finite beings "participate in being" (*esse commune*); their act of existence is limited *because* their particular essence is what it is. God's existence is not limited by an essence — his essence is identical to his existence (*ipsum esse subsistens*, "He Who Is"). "Exists" is said of God, of pebbles, of numbers analogically, not univocally.
- Closing formula: **"To be a Thomist therefore is always to be an Aristotelian, but also to go beyond Aristotle, just as Aquinas did."**

## Chapter 9 — Soul, body, and personal identity

- Aristotle: intellect (unlike perception) is not dependent on bodily sense organs — De Anima 429b4-5. Ibn Rushd (Averroes) read this as implying that what survives death is not the *individual* human being (though as a devout Muslim he held a bodily-resurrection view). Aquinas's *Quaestiones Disputatae de Anima* asks whether the soul can be **both a form and a particular thing** (*hoc aliquid*) — answered yes, because the soul has "its own peculiar activity," thinking, which does not require a bodily organ (no organ plays the role for thinking that eyes play for seeing) — but this does not collapse Aquinas's view into Plato's dualism.
- **What distinguishes Aquinas from Plato/Plotinus**: this particular thought is *mine* because of its relation to my particular body (whose interactions with the world supplied the perceptions from which thought begins) — my identity as thinker is *derivative from* my identity as an animal body, not prior to or independent of it. Consequently, a soul separated from the body at death is **incomplete** — "if soul is deprived of body, it will exist imperfectly" (Aquinas, commentary on 1 Corinthians, cited via McDermott 1993). This is why, for Aquinas, resurrection of the body — not merely survival of an immaterial soul — is required for full personal immortality: "soul is not the whole human being, only part of one: my soul is not me."

## Chapter 10 — Natural law, virtue, and the life of practice

- Aquinas's cardinal virtues (temperance, courage, justice, prudence) follow Plato; the detailed account of particular virtues follows Aristotle; the theological virtues (faith, hope, charity) and their relation to the natural virtues follow Augustine.
- **Law as teacher**: precepts of law are "precepts of reason directed toward the common good, enacted and promulgated by someone with authority to do so" (*S.Th.* Ia-IIae, 90). Four types: eternal law (God's rule of the universe), natural law (the part of eternal law humans grasp by reason), divine law (revealed — Old Law/Sinai, New Law/Sermon on the Mount), human law (enacted by rulers).
- **Primary vs. secondary precepts**: primary precepts (forbidding killing the innocent, theft, lying) are the same for everyone, everywhere, always. Secondary precepts (how to apply primary precepts to particular circumstances) vary by time, place, and available means.
- **MacIntyre's own argument for why the natural law precepts must be exactly these**: rational deliberation *in company with others* (needed to escape the one-sidedness of purely individual judgment) is only possible among people committed unconditionally to rules against killing, violence, theft, deception, and promise-breaking — precisely because without these, genuine (non-coercive, non-manipulative) rational deliberation with *anyone whatsoever* is impossible. This set of preconditions for rational deliberation turns out to be identical to Aquinas's primary precepts of natural law.
- The four cardinal virtues are needed to *apply* the precepts rightly (prudence especially); the theological virtues (unlike the cardinal virtues, infused by grace rather than acquired by habituation) are needed because purely secular moral effort cannot reach the final end (vision of God) or overcome recurrent failures of practical reason caused by disordered love of money, power, pleasure, fame.
- Aquinas's larger purpose in the *Summa Theologiae*'s moral sections (per Leonard Boyle O.P.) was to replace older confessional manuals used by parish priests — i.e., the *Summa* itself was written as an act of obedience to the very natural-law precept of contributing to the common good it expounds.

## Chapter 11 — Aquinas's philosophy of university education

- Aquinas's prescribed order of study: grammar/logic (skills) → mathematics (imagination, abstraction from experience) → natural science (return to experience) → moral/political philosophy (requires life-experience and passion-maturity the younger student lacks) → metaphysics and theology (requires "peculiar strength of intellect") — citing his *Commentary on the Nicomachean Ethics* VI, lect. VII.
- Purpose of this order: not administrative, but to develop students into **self-teachers** — independent theoretical and practical reasoners — by mirroring the order their own powers must develop in, culminating in the final end of human beings (perfected understanding, ultimately the vision of God). "We are able to understand what the university should be, only if we understand what the universe is."
- Historical note: this Thomistic conception of curriculum order was influential in Dominican teaching but did *not* shape how medieval universities in general actually developed, and Thomism did not become widely dominant until the 16th-century Spanish university revival.

## Bridge into Chapter 12 (context for the univocity/analogy debate)

Aquinas was a controversial, often-condemned figure in his own century (1270 and 1277 Paris condemnations by the bishop of Paris; a parallel 1277 Oxford condemnation targeting Aquinas's soul/body thesis specifically; William de la Mare's 1279 *Correctorium fratris Thomae* listing 117 theses; 1282 Franciscan ban on copying the *Summa* without the *Correctorium* attached). Duns Scotus's argument for the **univocity of being** was originally directed at Henry of Ghent, not explicitly at Aquinas, but some commentators wrongly treat it as also refuting Aquinas. MacIntyre's clarification: Aquinas's actual position is that "exists" and other God/creature terms are neither equivocal nor univocal but **analogical** — directly corroborating the Suárez/Scotus univocity-vs-analogy dispute already documented in [[Tomismo]] and [[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]] from the Garrigou-Lagrange sources.
