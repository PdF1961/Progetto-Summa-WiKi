## Entry: Accidente

From *accidere* (to happen/befall) — signals the accident's ontological non-self-sufficiency. Aristotle (*Metaphysics* E) divides being into substance and nine categories of accident (quality, quantity, action, relation, passion, place, time, situation, habit). Aquinas's *De natura accidentis*: accidents matter enormously epistemically, since all human knowledge starts from the senses, and accidents are sense's proper object — they give "great input toward knowing the essence of a thing" (*ad cognoscendum quod quid est*). Ontological status: accident is not devoid of being (it would be nothing), but has no act of existence of its own — it derives esse from substance, so esse is predicated of accident only **analogically**. Accident's mode of being is *inesse* ("to inhere"): "*Natura accidentis est inesse, sive inhaerere ipsi rei*." Hierarchy of inherence: quantity first, then quality, then place/relation/etc.; but quality and action can root directly in substance via form, bypassing quantity/matter.

**Theological application (Eucharist)**: Aquinas uses the real distinction between substance and accident to explain transubstantiation — the substance of bread/wine is wholly converted into the Body/Blood of Christ, while the accidents of bread/wine remain (*S.Th.* III, q.75, a.4). Two difficulties addressed: (1) *possibility* — God, as first cause, has an influence on the effects of secondary causes more fundamental than the secondary cause's own influence, so God can sustain accidents in being even once the substance (secondary cause) is removed; (2) *coherence* — of calling something an "accident" once it no longer *inhaeret* in a substance: Aquinas distinguishes *esse* from *modus essendi* — the eucharistic accidents' natural mode would be *inesse*, but what's essential to being an accident is not having one's own act of being, which remains true even when God directly sustains them without the substance.

## Entry: Essenza (essence)

- Aquinas's usage: essence/nature = what is included in the definition of the species (*I, q.3, a.3*); in composites of matter and form, essence signifies neither matter alone nor form alone but their union taken universally, as principles of the species (*I, q.29, a.2*). Synonyms in Aquinas: *natura*, *quidditas*, *quod quid est*, *substantia*, *species*.
- **Knowledge of essence**: achieved by intellectual apprehension (first intellectual operation, distinct from judgment), always via **abstraction** — Aquinas explicitly rejects both Platonic reminiscence and Augustinian illumination as accounts of essence-knowledge. Separate substances (angels/intelligences) apprehend essence directly; humans reach it discursively, through effects and properties, which is why human knowing is called *ratio* even though it terminates in *intellectus*.
- **Essence's metaphysical function**: to set limits on the (per se infinite) perfection of *esse* within a being — the limit is imposed neither by matter nor by form, but by essence itself. Mondin's example: a (wooden) bench, however finely made, can never have the perfection of consciousness or freedom, because its *essence as a bench* excludes them — while *ens* as *actualitas omnium actuum* contains them necessarily in itself. "*Essences are like vessels, containing as much esse as their capacity allows*" (paraphrasing *C.G.* I, c.43, n.363).
- This essence-as-limiting-principle insight let Aquinas dispense with **universal hylomorphism** (Avicebron's theory that matter enters into the constitution of every creature, including angels, since only matter's presence distinguishes creatures from God) — for Aquinas, finite essence alone suffices to explain angelic finitude.
- **The essence/esse composition is not the same kind of composition as matter/form** — Aquinas gives three reasons (*C.G.* II, c.54): (1) matter is not the substance itself (or all forms would be accidental, per the ancient naturalists); matter is *part* of the essence. (2) Esse is not act of matter alone but of the whole substance — "existing" is said of the composite, not of matter alone. (3) Even form is not esse — form relates to esse as light to shining; form is *principle* of esse (as complement of substance), while in separate/intellectual substances (no matter/form composition), form itself is what exists, and esse is both its act and that by which it exists — so there the *only* composition is of essence and esse.
- **Mondin's own interpretive thesis** (contra Gilson, Masnovo, Maritain, Fabro, who treat the real essence/esse distinction as *the* cardinal doctrine of Thomistic metaphysics): the true keystone is rather **esse conceived intensively** (as *actualitas omnium actuum*) — though he grants the real distinction is indispensable for avoiding Parmenides's error of absolutizing being to the point of erasing all distinction between beings.
- **Historical dispute among Thomists** on how "real" the distinction is: Egidio Romano over-reified it into two distinct *modes of being* (*esse essentiae* vs. *esse existentiae*); **Suárez** went the other way, reducing it to a merely logical distinction with some grounding in reality. Mondin: both are misreadings — for Aquinas it is a real but specifically **metaphysical** (not physical, unlike soul/body) distinction, unique in kind, holding only between essence and the act of existing.
- Textual loci cited for the real distinction: *I Sent.* d.19 q.2 a.2; *De Ver.* q.27 a.1 ad 8; *In De Hebd.* II, nn.33-34.

## Entry: Persona

- Etymology: from *personare* ("to resound," theatrical mask) — Aquinas notes the term's origin in the masks actors wore in tragedy/comedy (*I Sent.* d.23, q.1, a.1).
- **Historically, "person" is a Christian invention.** Neither Greek nor Latin had a word for the concept before Christianity, because classical culture recognized no absolute value in the individual as such — worth derived from class, wealth, race. Quoting R. Garaudy: "Christianity created a new dimension of man: that of person... a notion so foreign to classical rationalism that the Greek Fathers could not find in Greek philosophy the categories and words to express this new reality." The uniqueness and irrepeatable singularity of the person, and the consequent substantial equality in dignity of every human being, is a specifically Christian truth with (Mondin's phrase) "subversive power" — it gradually transformed pagan culture into the *respublica christiana* of the Middle Ages.
- **Augustine** (*De Trinitate*): sought a term applicable distinctly to Father, Son, and Spirit without either tritheism or dissolving their individuality. "Essence"/"substance" name what's common to all three; the Greek *hypostasis* and Latin *persona* name what is singular/individual. Applied analogically to man too: "*Singulus quisque homo... una persona est*."
- **Boethius's definition** (*Contra Eutychen et Nestorium*, c.4): "*persona est rationalis naturae individua substantia*" — an individual substance of rational nature. Mondin walks through why all four elements (rational, nature, individual, substance) are necessary: individuality alone belongs even to accidents; nature/substance alone can be generic (a rock or a cat has individual substance without being a person); only adding the specific difference of **rationality** yields person.
- **Aquinas's assessment**: adopts Boethius's definition (over rival ones by Richard of St. Victor and the "masters' definition" popular after Alan of Lille), refining it. Holds an extremely high view of personhood: "*Persona significat id quod est perfectissimum in tota natura*" (*I, q.28, a.3*) — person is the most perfect thing in the whole of nature, viewed ontologically as the mode in which esse (*actualitas omnium actuum*) achieves its fullest, most complete actuation. Hence all beings entitled "person" — God, angels, man — possess infinite dignity and absolute value.
- **Person is said analogically**, not univocally, of God, angels, and man (*secundum prius et posterius*) — always designating the same fundamental perfection: individual subsistence in the order of spirit. "*Omne subsistens in natura rationali vel intellectuali est persona*" (*C.G.* IV, c.35). In the definition's term "substantia," Aquinas specifies it means *prima substantia* (the singular subject/*suppositum*), not *secunda substantia* (universal essence) — since person is always a singular subsistent.
