## Core argument (per MacIntyre's own 2006 restatement)

Modern moral culture presents itself as a battlefield of unresolved and apparently unresolvable disputes (utilitarians vs. Kantians vs. contractarians) precisely because moral rules that once made sense within a context of shared practices and a shared conception of the human good have survived as **fragments**, stripped of that context, and so reduced in practice to expressions of the speaker's own attitude — MacIntyre's **emotivism** thesis (chs. 2–3). The Enlightenment project (chs. 4–6) tried to justify morality for "man-as-he-happens-to-be" while discarding the Aristotelian teleological "man-as-he-could-be-if-he-realized-his-telos" — and could not succeed, because moral rules only made sense as the bridge between those two conceptions.

The recovery MacIntyre proposes (chs. 9–18) runs through Homeric heroic societies, Athens, and Aristotle's own account of virtue, to a specifically medieval synthesis (ch. 13, where MacIntyre — not yet a Thomist when he wrote this — first engages Aquinas), before constructing his own positive account across chs. 14–15: virtues as what enable achievement of goods **internal to practices** ([[Pratica e Beni Interni]]), ordered by the **narrative unity of a single human life**, situated within a living moral **tradition** ([[Unità Narrativa della Vita e Tradizione]]). Chapter 9's "Nietzsche or Aristotle?" frames the stakes: if the Enlightenment project's failure is total, the only honest alternatives are Nietzschean genealogical unmasking of all morality as will-to-power, or a return to something like the Aristotelian tradition of the virtues — MacIntyre argues for the latter.

The book closes (ch. 18) with its famous image: the crisis of moral modernity as analogous to the fall of the classical world, and the suggestion that "we are waiting... for another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict," i.e. for a renewal of the local, practice-sustaining forms of community the old moral order depended on (see [[Unità Narrativa della Vita e Tradizione]] for MacIntyre's own 2006 gloss on this line).

## The 2006 Italian preface: MacIntyre's turn to Thomism (directly relevant to this vault)

Read in full. Key points, in MacIntyre's own words:

- When he wrote *After Virtue* he was "already an Aristotelian thinker, but not yet a Thomist" — visible in how he treated Aquinas only briefly at the end of chapter 13.
- He became a Thomist afterward, for three reasons: (1) his account of the social good via practices/traditions/narrative unity needed an explicit **metaphysical** foundation, which he found already implicitly assumed and which Aquinas supplies explicitly in *Summa Theologiae* Ia, q.5 (the doctrine of the good); (2) he needed a non-necessarily-Aristotelian **biological** grounding for attributing virtue/vice to humans (developed later in *Dependent Rational Animals*, 1999, via human kinship with other animal species); (3) Aquinas's doctrine of ***misericordia*** (mercy) as one of the **"virtues of acknowledged dependence"** — a point on which Aquinas decisively parts from Aristotle.
- Restates and defends the **tradition-constituted rationality** epistemology against the recurring charge of relativism (see [[Unità Narrativa della Vita e Tradizione]]) — explicitly listing the Aristotelian-Thomist tradition, Madhyamaka Buddhism, and Anglo-American utilitarianism as three long-coexisting rival traditions of moral enquiry.
- Explicitly rejects being classed with liberal-vs-communitarian "communitarianism" (naming Amitai Etzioni), and states that his critique of liberalism is not a defense of conservatism, which he judges an equally individualist mirror image of liberalism.
- Identifies chapter 3's gallery of modernity's stock characters (the Manager, the Therapist, the Aesthete) as needing, in retrospect, a fourth addition: the blustering **contemporary moralist conservative**.
