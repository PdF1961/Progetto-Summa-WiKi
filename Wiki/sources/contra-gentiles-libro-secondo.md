# Source: Summa contro i Gentili, Libro II — deep-ingest (ch.56-59, 79: the human soul)

**Pages**: Libro II runs approximately pp.266-549 of the raw PDF ("LIBRO SECONDO" running header first at p.266; "LIBRO TERZO" first at p.550). The soul/body material read in this batch clusters at pp.393-433 (substantial form; the anti-Averroist polemic) and pp.468-473 (immortality).

## Soul as substantial form of the body (ch.56-58)

Ch.56 opens by posing the difficulty at full strength: how can an intellective substance — already established earlier in Book II as existing *in actu* and immaterial in its own right — also be the *substantial form* of a body, given that (1) two actually-existing substances can't compose a single thing, (2) form and matter belong to the same genus while intellective substance and body don't, (3) a form's being is *in* matter, which would make intellective substance material after all, contradicting what was already proven, and (4) philosophers demonstrate the intellect is separate from body entirely. These four objections are stated in full before being answered — a genuine dialectical setup, not a foregone conclusion.

**Ch.57** takes up Plato's alternative directly: that the intellective soul unites to the body not as form to matter but only as **mover to moved**, "come il nocchiero nella nave" (like a pilot in a ship — Aquinas's own citation of Plato's *Alcibiades* I via Aristotle's *De Anima* report). Aquinas's refutation: mere motive contact doesn't produce an *absolute* unity, only an accidental one — but the union of soul and body constitutes *the man himself*, so on Plato's model man would not be a being *per se* but only *per accidens* (an aggregate, like clothing worn by a wearer), which contradicts the evident unity of human experience. Plato's own fallback — that man just *is* the soul, using the body as an instrument, "così come Pietro non è il composto di un uomo e del suo vestito, ma è l'uomo" — is set up here for refutation in the chapter's continuation (not re-read in full in this pass).

## The anti-Averroist polemic on the unity of the intellect (ch.59)

This is the standout discovery of this batch: an extended, dedicated chapter refuting the Averroist doctrine that all humans share **one single separate possible intellect** — a doctrine [[god-philosophy-universities]] alludes to but doesn't quote directly, and which this vault had no primary-text location for before now. Averroes's position, as Aquinas states it: the possible intellect is "separato," "non mescolato al corpo," "semplice," "impassibile" (citing Aristotle's own language), and connects to individual humans only accidentally, via the *phantasm* acting as a kind of shared interface — "a noi codesto intelletto si unisce mediante il fantasma, che è quasi il subietto di tale forma intelligibile."

Aquinas calls this "frivolo ed impossibile" and gives five arguments against it in sequence, the sharpest being: if the possible intellect is united to us only via the *object* known (the phantasm) rather than as our own form, then strictly speaking **we would not be the ones understanding** — we would merely be *known* by a separate intelligence, the way an object is known by a knower, rather than ourselves being intelligent subjects: *"l'uomo non otterrà d'intendere, ma solo di essere conosciuto intellettualmente da un intelletto separato."* This is the primary-text root of the personal-identity stakes [[god-philosophy-universities]] ch.9 raises about Aquinas's soul/body doctrine (the reason resurrection of the body, not mere survival of an impersonal intellect, is needed for *personal* immortality) — Averroism is precisely the view where "I" never actually think at all, only a separate cosmic intellect does, using my phantasms as its raw material.

## The soul's immortality (ch.79)

*"L'anima umana non viene distrutta con la distruzione del corpo"* — building on ch.55's prior result that all intellective substances are incorruptible. The argument given: corruption belongs to things whose perfection consists in what corruption destroys, but the soul's perfection consists precisely in a certain *abstraction* from bodily reality (perfected by knowledge and virtue, both of which improve in proportion to detachment from bodily passion) — so separation from the body cannot be what destroys the soul; if anything it is what perfects it. Aquinas also blocks a fallback objection (that the soul might perfect its *operation* by separating from the body while its *substance* is destroyed by that same separation): operation manifests substance directly ("ogni essere agisce in quanto è"), so whatever perfects a thing's operation cannot simultaneously destroy its substance.

## What remains unread

Chapters 46-55 (the soul's origin, its distinction from other forms, the proof that intellective substances are incorruptible presupposed by ch.79), 58 (the continuation of the anti-Platonic argument), 60-78 (the bulk of the anti-Averroist material — ch.59 is only the opening salvo of what is, per standard accounts, a nearly twenty-chapter polemic), and 80-90 (further immortality arguments, the soul's multiplication/individuation, and the transition to Book III) are confirmed to exist via the surrounding page range but not read in this pass. Given this vault's existing MacIntyre coverage, chs.60-78 (the fuller Averroist polemic) are the natural next target if this treatise is revisited.
