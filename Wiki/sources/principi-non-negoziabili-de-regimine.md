# Source: De regimine principum ad regem Cypri

**Note on the title**: the .epub file bears the modern marketing title "Principi non negoziabili sulla società e sulla politica" (echoing the "non-negotiable principles" phrase from early-2000s Catholic political discourse), but the actual text — confirmed by direct reading — is the authentic medieval treatise **De regimine principum ad regem Cypri** ("On Kingship, to the King of Cyprus"), also known as *De regno*. This is a real, well-attested Aquinas work (cross-referenced independently in [[le-thomisme-gilson]]'s works catalogue, dated 1265-1266). A duplicate copy of this same epub's content also exists as a PDF at `.raw/S. Tommaso d'Aquino - Principi non Negoziabili sulla Società e sulla Politica.pdf` — not separately ingested since it is the same text.

**Authorship note**: per Gilson's bibliography (cross-checked in [[le-thomisme-gilson]]), only **Book I entire and Book II chapters 1-4** are authentically Aquinas's — he left the work unfinished (probably interrupted by other duties or his death in 1274); the remainder (Book II chs. 5-16, Books III-IV) was completed by his fellow Dominican **Tolomeo da Lucca (Ptolemy of Lucca)**.

**Format**: epub, ~79,000 words, 4 books (Libro I: 15 chapters; Libro II: 16 chapters; Libro III: 22 chapters; Libro IV: 23 chapters — 76 chapters total)

## Reading note

Read in full: Book I, chapters 1-4 (the authentically Thomistic core of the argument for monarchy and against tyranny). The remaining 72 chapters (rest of Book I, all of Books II-IV) are recorded from the verified chapter list only.

## Core argument (Book I, chs. 1-4, read in full)

**Ch. 1 — Why any society needs a ruler**: man, unlike solitary animals, is by nature a "social and political animal" (echoing Aristotle) who needs others to survive — nature gives him reason instead of claws, fur, or speed, but no single man's reason suffices to discover everything needed for life, so humans must live in society, each contributing different expertise, coordinated through speech (more expressive than any animal signal). But a multitude of individuals each pursuing private good would fragment without someone attending to the **common good** — "where a ruler is lacking, the people will be scattered" (Prov. 11:14).

**Ch. 2 — Why rule by one is better than rule by many**: since the ruler's aim must be the preservation of the multitude's unity (peace), and "what is one in itself can secure unity better than several individuals" (as what is hot by nature is the most efficient cause of heating), rule by one is more effective at achieving the end of government than rule by many. Confirmed by nature's own pattern: one heart moves all the body's members, one faculty (reason) presides over the soul, bees have one king, and "in the whole universe one God is Creator and ruler of all things." Also confirmed by experience: provinces/cities not ruled by one are "troubled by dissent," those ruled by a single king "enjoy peace and flourish in justice."

**Ch. 3 — Why, by the same logic, tyranny is the worst regime**: Aquinas's six-regime taxonomy — monarchy/tyranny (rule of one), aristocracy/oligarchy (rule of few), *politia*/democracy (rule of many) — pairs just and unjust versions. Since monarchy is the best just regime, tyranny (its corrupt mirror) is the worst: a unified force does more good when good, but also more harm when evil, than a divided one — so tyranny is worse than oligarchy, oligarchy worse than democracy. Extensive analysis of tyranny's characteristic behaviors: plundering subjects' goods (if ruled by greed), shedding blood over trifles (if ruled by wrath), and above all suppressing the conditions of solidarity among subjects — tyrants "sow discord among subjects, foment quarrels, forbid marriages, banquets, and anything that builds alliance and trust" between people, specifically **because** they fear subjects' virtue, wealth, and mutual trust could be turned against their illegitimate rule. Cites Job, Proverbs, Ezekiel, Ecclesiastes, Colossians, Aristotle, Cicero, and Sallust throughout. Note: an editorial footnote flags that ch. 2's praise of monarchy and ch. 3's condemnation of one-man rule are not contradictory — ch. 2 addresses political rule in a normally well-ordered society, ch. 3 addresses a dissolute one.

**Ch. 4 — Historical case studies**: Rome's history (expulsion of kings → consuls/aristocracy, per Sallust's observation that liberty made Rome grow "incredibly fast," because citizens under one-man rule serve the common good less zealously than when the common good is visibly their own → later civil wars and reversion to emperors, some ruling well "as kings," many degenerating into tyranny) and the Hebrew people's history (judges period: fragmented, "everyone did what seemed right in his own eyes," constantly preyed upon by enemies → asked God for kings → some kings' wickedness led Israel into idolatry and slavery). Aquinas's conclusion: real danger exists on **both** sides — fear of tyranny may lead people to reject good kingship, while desiring a king risks the good regime degenerating into tyrannical perfidy.

## Structure (verified chapter count, remaining chapters not yet read)

- **Libro I** (15 chapters, authentically Aquinas) — chs. 1-4 above; remaining chapters (not yet read) continue the kingship/tyranny analysis and (per the standard content of *De regno*) address the king's duties, reward in the afterlife, and the analogy of royal governance to divine governance of the universe.
- **Libro II** (16 chapters; only chs. 1-4 authentically Aquinas) — the transition point where Ptolemy of Lucca's continuation begins.
- **Libro III** (22 chapters, Ptolemy of Lucca)
- **Libro IV** (23 chapters, Ptolemy of Lucca)

## Significance for this vault

A genuine primary-source complement to [[god-philosophy-universities]] ch. 10's account of Aquinas's natural law theory: where MacIntyre's secondary account describes natural law's precepts abstractly, this text shows Aquinas's own applied political reasoning — the common good as what distinguishes just from unjust rule, and a strikingly concrete phenomenology of how tyranny operates by deliberately destroying the trust and solidarity natural law and virtue would otherwise produce among citizens.
