## Structure, confirmed from each question's own prologue

Q91's prologue (a.1) lays out the six kinds of law to be treated in sequence: eternal, natural, human, divine (a.4-5), and "the law of the fomes" (concupiscence, a.6, i.e. *lex peccati* — not really a law but named by analogy). Q94-97 then treat natural and human law in depth; q.98-105 treat the Old Law (general questions q.98, then precepts divided into moral/q.100, ceremonial/qq.101-103, and judicial/qq.104-105 — Aquinas's own three-way division of the Old Law's 613 precepts); q.106-108 treat the New Law. This confirms the standard scholarly account already assumed in [[somma-teologica-struttura]] and cross-referenced by [[god-philosophy-universities]] ch.10, now verified article-by-article from the text's own prologues.

## The definition of law (q.90 a.1)

Against the objection that law belongs to will, not reason (citing the jurists' maxim *"ciò che piace al principe ha vigore di legge"*), Aquinas grounds law in reason: *"La legge è una regola o misura dell'agire... Ora, la misura degli atti umani è la ragione, che ne è il primo principio... Quindi la legge è qualcosa che appartiene alla ragione."* Law is a measure of action, and reason — not will alone — is what measures human acts, because reason's proper task is ordering to an end, "il primo principio in campo operativo." This is the article underlying the classic four-part definition (*ordinatio rationis ad bonum commune, ab eo qui curam communitatis habet, promulgata* — an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by whoever has care of the community, and promulgated), whose four elements are established one per article in q.90 (reason: a.1; common good: a.2; competent authority: a.3; promulgation: a.4).

## Eternal law as the ground of natural law (q.91 a.2)

Q.91 a.1 first establishes an eternal law exists (God's practical reason governing the universe, per the providence doctrine already read in [[somma-teologica-de-deo-uno]] q.22). Then the article on natural law states directly: *"la legge non è che il dettame della ragione pratica esistente nel principe che governa una società... tutta la comunità dell'universo è governata dalla ragione divina. Perciò il piano stesso col quale Dio, come principe dell'universo, governa le cose ha natura di legge... tale legge deve essere eterna."* Natural law (a.2, not fully re-quoted here) is then defined as the rational creature's own *participation* in this eternal law — the textual root of the "law is reason's dictate, and natural law is our share in God's own eternal ordering" doctrine central to Thomistic natural law theory as presented in [[god-philosophy-universities]] ch.10.

## The primary precepts and the three-tier natural inclinations (q.94 a.2)

This is the single most load-bearing passage in the treatise. Natural law's precepts follow the order of natural inclinations, in three tiers:

1. **Shared with all substances**: self-preservation — *"ogni sostanza tende per natura alla conservazione del proprio essere"* — so natural law includes whatever preserves human life and prevents its destruction.
2. **Shared with other animals**: *"le cose che la natura ha insegnato a tutti gli animali"* (quoting the *Digest*) — the union of male and female, care of offspring, and similar.
3. **Proper to reason, specifically human**: the inclination to know the truth about God and to live in society — *"la fuga dell'ignoranza, il rispetto di coloro con cui si deve convivere e altre cose del genere."*

Aquinas is explicit that this plurality of precepts remains **one** natural law, unified by reference back to the single first precept (*bonum est faciendum et prosequendum, malum vitandum* — good is to be done and pursued, evil avoided, established earlier in the article, not re-quoted in full here but presupposed by this passage's "quindi da farsi... da evitarsi" phrasing) — reason apprehends as good, and hence to-be-done, whatever a natural inclination tends toward, and apprehends the contrary as evil, hence to-be-avoided. This three-tier structure is the primary-text anchor for any future comparison against [[god-philosophy-universities]] ch.10's MacIntyre-mediated account of natural law, not yet cross-checked article-by-article in this pass.

## The New Law as principally interior grace (q.106)

Q.106's own prologue frames the whole New Law treatise around one question first: *"La sua qualità, se cioè questa legge sia scritta o infusa"* — is the New Law a written code or an infused reality? Article 1 poses this sharply through objections (the New Law just *is* the Gospel, and the Gospel is written scripture — "queste cose sono state scritte perché crediate," Jn 20:31) against the *in contrario* citation of Jeremiah/Hebrews on the new covenant "written on the heart." The article's *Dimostrazione* (not captured in this pass, flagged for follow-up) is where Aquinas gives his famous answer: the New Law consists **principally** in the grace of the Holy Spirit given through faith in Christ, working inwardly, with the written Gospel text serving only a secondary, instrumental role (*"lex nova... est principaliter gratia ipsa Spiritus Sancti"* in the standard Latin) — a doctrine already known to this vault only via [[Teologia Morale nella Sintesi Tomistica]]'s Garrigou-Lagrange-mediated summary, now located precisely in the primary text for future direct verification.

## What remains unread

qq.92-93, 95, 97 (human law's utility/origin/mutability), the bulk of the Old Law's ceremonial (qq.101-103) and judicial (qq.104-105) precept material, and qq.106 aa.2-4/107/108 (New Law's justifying power, its relation to the Old Law, and its content — the evangelical counsels) are confirmed structurally via their own prologues (quoted/paraphrased above) but not read article-by-article. Given this vault's Thomistic focus, q.94 (natural law, now well-covered) and q.96 a.4 (binding force / unjust law) are the highest-priority remaining targets for a future short follow-up.
