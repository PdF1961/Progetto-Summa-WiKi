# Source: Opuscoli teologico-spirituali — the Naples Lenten sermons of 1273

**Author**: San Tommaso d'Aquino
**Original titles**: *In symbolum apostolorum ("Credo in Deum") expositio*; *In orationem dominicam ("Pater noster") expositio*; *In duo praecepta caritatis et in decem legis praecepta expositio*; *In salutationem angelicam ("Ave Maria") expositio*
**Format**: OCR'd PDF, ~73,000 words

## What this source is

Four opuscula that are stenographic transcriptions of roughly 58-59 sermons Aquinas delivered, in his last year of life (1273), from the pulpit of San Domenico Maggiore in Naples — to his university students and to the lay population of the city, in **vernacular** (his native dialect, likely Neapolitan/Campanian, not the technical Latin of academic disputation). This makes the collection uniquely valuable as a window onto Aquinas's *pastoral*, exoteric voice, contrasted with the dense scholastic register of the *Summa* or the disputed questions.

## Historical context (per the volume's Introduzione, read in full)

- Aquinas was ~48 and, per the editor's speculation, may already have sensed the illness that would fell him within the year (he died 7 March 1274, en route to the Council of Lyon).
- Structure follows the three theological virtues, as Aquinas himself states opening the Decalogue cycle: "To attain salvation, man must know some basic things: what he must believe, what he must desire, and finally what he must do. The first need is answered by the Creed, which gathers the fundamental articles of revelation; the second by the Our Father; the third by God's law" — i.e. faith / hope / charity respectively.
- Delivery breakdown (editor's reconstruction from the stenographic record): 15 sermons on the Creed, 10 on the Our Father, 32 on the Decalogue (compared against the two precepts of charity), and 1-2 on the Hail Mary — roughly 58-59 total, delivered across ~38 days of actual preaching starting Monday of Septuagesima (6 February 1273), constrained by the liturgical calendar (preaching was impossible during the *Tenebrae* offices of the last days of Holy Week).
- Authenticated as genuine transcription (not Aquinas's own written notes, unlike his earlier *Dominicales*) by Fra Pietro da Andria's testimony and by witnesses at the 1319 Naples and Fossanova canonization proceedings.
- The editor discusses at length (and inconclusively) the linguistic question of which vernacular Aquinas actually preached in, weighing his Neapolitan mother Teodora, his Campanian childhood, his years in Cassino/Naples/Cologne/Paris, and comparative philological evidence from period Neapolitan texts (the *Cronaca di Partenope*, a dialect letter of Boccaccio's).

## Sample of content: the Credo's introduction — a pastoral apologetic for faith

Read in full. Aquinas opens not with technical epistemology but a direct, pastoral case for why faith matters and is reasonable, addressed to ordinary listeners:

1. Faith unites the soul to God (citing Hosea 2:22's spiritual marriage image) — baptism without faith ("do you believe in God?") does not profit.
2. Faith is the seed of eternal life, itself defined as fully knowing God (John 17:3) — imperfect now, perfected in the beatific vision.
3. Faith is the surest guide for right living — without it, reason alone would take far too long (or never arrive) at the basic rules of rectitude; "even before Christ, no sage, however brilliant, attained as much certainty about God and the means to eternal life as a simple Christian old woman possesses through faith."
4. Faith overcomes temptation (from the devil, the world, and the flesh), each answered by a specific scriptural assurance.

**On the reasonableness of believing what is not directly seen**: Aquinas's answer to the objection that faith is foolish — no philosopher has ever fully fathomed the nature of a single fly (citing a scholar who spent thirty years studying bee behavior); if human intellect is this weak even about trivial creatures, it is no folly to accept elementary truths about God via revelation rather than demanding full rational comprehension of the "mysterious" and "uncountable" (Job 36:26). Further: since angelic intellect vastly exceeds the sharpest philosopher (more than a master exceeds an ignorant layman), it would be unwise to refuse credit to a truth an angel reports — far more so a truth God himself reveals. And practically: no one could live at all while accepting only what is directly experienced (how would anyone know their own father, without trusting testimony?) — so trusting God's testimony, the most credible of all, is simply the general practice of trusting testimony taken to its proper limit. Finally, **miracles function as God's authenticating "seal"** on revealed truth, just as a king's seal authenticates his letters — Christ validated the teaching of the apostles and hagiographers with miracles beyond mere creaturely power.

## Significance for this vault

This is the vault's first genuinely **primary, pastoral-register** Aquinas text (as opposed to secondary scholarly treatments in Garrigou-Lagrange, MacIntyre, Mondin, Gilson). It complements [[Tomismo]] and [[San Tommaso d'Aquino]] by showing how the same thinker who produces the dense metaphysics of essence/esse and the five ways also produced accessible catechesis for ordinary lay Neapolitans, entirely in the vernacular, in the last year of his life.

## Flagged for follow-up deep ingest

Only the Credo's introduction has been read in full; the article-by-article commentary on each clause of the Creed, the full Pater Noster exposition, the Decalogue/two-precepts-of-charity exposition (the longest section, ~32 sermons), and the Ave Maria commentary remain unread — strong candidates for a dedicated follow-up pass, and a natural primary-source complement to [[god-philosophy-universities]]'s treatment of Aquinas's natural law doctrine.
