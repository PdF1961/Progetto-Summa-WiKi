# Pratica e Beni Interni (Practice and Internal Goods)

Core technical concept from Alasdair MacIntyre's [[dopo-la-virtu]] chapter 14, the first of three stages (practice → narrative unity of a life → tradition) in his account of the virtues.

## Definition

A **practice** is "any coherent and complex form of socially established cooperative human activity through which goods internal to that form of activity are realized in the course of trying to achieve those standards of excellence which are appropriate to, and partially definitive of, that form of activity, with the result that human powers to achieve excellence, and human conceptions of the ends and goods involved, are systematically extended."

- Chess is a practice; merely throwing a ball skillfully is not, but the game of football is.
- Bricklaying is not a practice; architecture is. Planting turnips is not; farming is.
- Physics, chemistry, biology, historical inquiry, painting, music, and (in the ancient/medieval world) the founding and sustaining of human communities (households, cities, nations) all count.

## Internal vs. external goods (the chess example)

MacIntyre's illustration: a seven-year-old is taught chess by being paid in candy for playing and for winning. As long as candy is his only reason to play, he has every reason to cheat whenever he can get away with it. But if he comes to value the specific goods of chess itself — analytic skill, strategic imagination, competitive intensity — he now has reason not to cheat, because cheating would defeat only himself.

- **External goods** (prestige, status, money, and — for the child — candy) attach to a practice only contingently, via particular social circumstances, and can always be achieved by alternative means (there is more than one way to get rich or famous).
- **Internal goods** can only be specified in terms of the practice itself and identified/recognized only by the experience of participating in it; achieving them enriches the whole community that shares the practice, unlike external goods, which are typically competed for as someone's possession at others' expense.

## Significance for the theory of virtue

Practices supply the first, necessary-but-incomplete stage of MacIntyre's account: a **virtue** is (provisionally) an acquired human quality the possession and exercise of which tends to enable us to achieve goods internal to practices, and the lack of which effectively prevents us from doing so. This stage is completed only by the second stage, the **narrative unity of a single human life**, and the third, **tradition** — see [[Unità Narrativa della Vita e Tradizione]].
