# Dottrina di Atto e Potenza

The Aristotelian-Thomistic doctrine that being divides into **potency** (real capacity for perfection, distinct from mere logical possibility) and **act** (perfection actually possessed), with the primacy of act over potency. Presented systematically in [[essenza-e-attualita-del-tomismo]] Part II (originally a paper at the 1925 International Thomistic Congress).

## Why the distinction is needed

Resolves Parmenides's argument against all change and multiplicity (from the principle of non-contradiction, "being is being, non-being is non-being," Parmenides concluded being is one, undivided, unique). Aristotle's solution: what becomes was not simply non-being nor simply being-in-act, but being **in potency**. E.g., a statue comes not from the statue (already act) nor from nothing/negation, but from wood having real capacity to receive the statue's form.

Aquinas refines Aristotle's real passive potency by distinguishing it from mere logical possibility (which suffices for creation *ex nihilo* but not for change/*mutatio*, which presupposes a determinable subject).

## Core principle

**"Actus non limitatur nisi per potentiam in qua recipitur"** — act, as perfection, is unlimited in itself and is limited only by the potency (real capacity) in which it is received. Not provable directly (it is a per se nota principle once the terms are explained), but defended by an indirect/reductio argument: an intrinsic limiting principle for act cannot be an extrinsic agent-cause; it must be an internally proportioned potency.

## Chain of applications (ascending way, from sensible things to God)

1. Matter is really distinct from form (substantial change requires pure potency as subject).
2. **Finite/created essence is really distinct from its act of existence (esse)** — the central thesis. Only God is *ipsum esse subsistens*; every creature merely *has* esse, received and limited by essence.
3. Only in God are essence and existence identical — the "supreme truth of Christian philosophy" per Aquinas, and (per Garrigou-Lagrange) the metaphysical ground of God's infinity and real distinction from the world.

## Applications (descending way, from God to creatures)

- God as Pure Act can have no accident (nothing can be added to Him); creatures, not being their own esse, can receive accidents.
- No infinite regress in a series of essentially subordinated efficient causes actually existing and operating together (grounds the "first way").
- The rational soul is the sole substantial form of the human body (against a plurality-of-forms view) — union of soul and body must be as potency to its correlative act to yield a true substantial unity (*unum per se*), not an accidental aggregate.
- Individuation is by "signate matter" (*materia quantitate signata*); pure spirits (angels) cannot multiply within a species.
- Grounds the real distinction between substance and quantity used in the Eucharistic doctrine (quantity of bread can remain without the substance of bread).

## Polemical target: potency as "imperfect act"

Garrigou-Lagrange's central complaint against **Suárez** (and, differently, **Leibniz**) is treating potency as a degree of imperfect act rather than a really distinct capacity. Consequence: the demonstrative force of the proofs of God's existence is reduced to mere probability, and Parmenides's/Spinoza's argument against the plurality of beings is never actually answered — only bypassed via an inductive argument from contingency (Suárez's route), which Garrigou-Lagrange judges weaker, especially for proving angels are created (no empirical generation/corruption to observe, unlike bodies).

## Further principles derived from being (per [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Prima)

Below the potency/act principle itself, being (*ens*, first object of the intellect) grounds a family of correlative principles, all analogical rather than univocal in application:

- **Principle of sufficient reason** (*ragion d'essere*): everything has its reason for being, in itself (if it exists per se) or in another. Four analogical senses: (1) a thing's properties in its essence/nature, (2) an effect's existence in its efficient cause, (3) means in their end, (4) matter as reason for a body's corruptibility.
- **Principle of substance**: what exists as subject of existence (*id quod est*) is substance, distinct from its accidents/modes (*id quo aliquid est*) — a determination of the principle of identity.
- **Principle of efficient causality**, reformulated away from a Humean "every phenomenon presupposes an antecedent phenomenon" toward "everything that comes into existence has an efficient cause" / "every contingent being, even if it existed *ab aeterno*, needs an efficient cause and ultimately an uncaused cause."
- **Principle of finality**: every agent acts for an end — naturally/unconsciously (a stone falling), via sensitive cognition (an animal seeking food), or via intellect grasping the end precisely *as* end (*sub ratione finis*). Grounds the first principle of practical reason: do good, avoid evil.

## Obediential potency (potenza obbedienziale)

A further supernatural-order application: the creature's fitness to receive a supernatural gift or, elevated, to produce a supernatural effect. For Aquinas this is a **passive** potency (e.g. the soul's, intellect's, will's own nature, apt for elevation), requiring only non-repugnance — God can do in us anything that is not self-contradictory. Suárez treats it as **active**, which Garrigou-Lagrange judges incoherent: an active obediential potency would be simultaneously "essentially natural" (a property of nature) and "essentially supernatural" (specified by a supernatural formal object) — a confusion of orders. Consequence for Christology: because Suárez denies the real essence/esse distinction, he is committed to **two acts of existence in Christ**, weakening the hypostatic union; for Aquinas there is a single esse in Christ, communicated by the Word to the assumed human nature — and, analogously, a single uncreated esse (*ipsum esse subsistens*) for the three Persons of the Trinity.

## Essence as limiting principle, and disputes over the real distinction (per [[mondin-dizionario-tommaso]])

Mondin's account sharpens *why* essence, specifically, is the limiting principle of esse (rather than matter or form): essence functions like a "vessel," containing only as much esse as its own capacity allows — a wooden bench, however finely made, can never possess the perfection of consciousness or freedom, because *its essence as a bench* excludes them, even though *ens* as such (*actualitas omnium actuum*) contains such perfections necessarily in itself. This let Aquinas dispense with **universal hylomorphism** (Avicebron's view that matter enters into every creature, including angels, since only matter distinguishes creature from God): finite essence alone suffices to explain angelic finitude.

Mondin also flags a **historical dispute among Thomists themselves** over how to read the real distinction: **Egidio Romano** over-reified it into two distinct modes of being (*esse essentiae* vs. *esse existentiae*); **Suárez** went the opposite way, reducing it to a merely logical distinction with some grounding in reality. Mondin judges both readings mistaken — for Aquinas it is a real but uniquely **metaphysical** distinction (unlike the physical distinction of soul/body), holding only between essence and the act of existing.

**Mondin's own interpretive thesis**, offered against Gilson, Masnovo, Maritain, and Fabro (who treat the real essence/esse distinction as *the* cardinal doctrine of Thomistic metaphysics): the true keystone is rather **esse conceived intensively** (as *actualitas omnium actuum*, the actuality of all acts) — though he grants the real distinction remains indispensable for avoiding Parmenides's error of absolutizing being to the point of erasing all distinction between beings.
