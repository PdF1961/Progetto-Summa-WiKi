# Unità Narrativa della Vita e Tradizione

Stages two and three of Alasdair MacIntyre's three-stage account of the virtues in [[dopo-la-virtu]] (stage one is [[Pratica e Beni Interni]]).

## Stage two: narrative unity of a single human life

A human life has intelligibility only as a narrative: actions are made sense of by situating them within an ongoing story that has a beginning, middle, and (anticipated) end, and a *telos* toward which it moves. The virtues needed to sustain a practice over a whole life, and to order the goods of many different practices against each other, presuppose this narrative unity — otherwise the self dissolves into a series of disconnected episodes without a standpoint from which to ask "what is the good life for me?"

## Stage three: tradition

A single life's narrative is itself intelligible only situated within a **tradition** — an historically extended, socially embodied argument about the goods that constitute that tradition, precisely because those goods and the standards of excellence used to identify them are themselves contested and evolve through time. Traditions in good order are constituted by continuous argument over their own fundamental goods.

## Tradition-constituted rationality (epistemology of traditions)

Elaborated further in *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988) and restated in MacIntyre's 2006 preface to the Italian edition of [[dopo-la-virtu]]: there is no tradition-independent, neutral standpoint from which to adjudicate between rival traditions of enquiry (MacIntyre's examples: Aristotelian-Thomism, Madhyamaka Buddhism, Anglo-American utilitarianism). Yet this does **not** entail relativism. A tradition can be judged rationally superior to a rival if:

1. Its adherents first do the imaginative work of learning to think as a fully committed adherent of the rival tradition would.
2. They identify, by the rival tradition's *own* internal criteria, the questions and problems that tradition cannot solve while remaining faithful to its own premises (an *epistemological crisis*).
3. Their own tradition's resources can explain why the rival is stuck **and** supply the resources to resolve the impasse — resources the rival tradition could not generate internally.

This lets a tradition claim superior truth and rational justification without any neutral criterion "outside" all traditions — which is precisely why the disagreement between rival traditions can remain unresolved for long periods (as, historically, between Aristotelian-Thomism, Buddhism, and utilitarianism) without that persistence proving relativism true.

## Connection to Thomism

MacIntyre explicitly frames the **Aristotelian-Thomist tradition** as one instance of a tradition of rational enquiry among others (not a privileged exception), whose claim to truth must be — and in his view can be — defended by the above method rather than by external fiat. This is the methodological bridge from *After Virtue*'s historical narrative to his later, explicitly Thomist work — see [[Alasdair MacIntyre]] for the three specific doctrinal points (metaphysics of the good, biological grounding, *misericordia*) on which Aquinas led him beyond the 1981 book's Aristotelianism.

## The book's famous closing image

*Dopo la virtù* ends by suggesting that the crisis of moral modernity resembles the collapse of the classical world before the rise of monasticism, and that "we are waiting not for a Godot, but for another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict." MacIntyre's own gloss (2006 preface): Benedict's greatness was making possible the monastery — centered on prayer, study, and work — in and around which communities could not just survive but develop new forms of common life through a period of social and cultural darkness. The 1980 sentence intended to suggest that modernity is likewise a time of waiting for unforeseeable possibilities of renewal, combined with prudent, courageous, just, and temperate resistance to the dominant social, economic, and political order of advanced modernity.
