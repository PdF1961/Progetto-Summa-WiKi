# Gerarchia degli Esseri e Analogia

Étienne Gilson's synthesis, from the concluding chapter ("L'esprit du thomisme") of [[le-thomisme-gilson]], of how the Thomistic cosmos hangs together as a single ordered whole.

## Being as the root of the transcendentals

The whole system is woven from a small number of principles that are, at bottom, aspects of one idea: **being** (*l'être*). A being is one insofar as it is undivided from itself; it grounds truth insofar as to speak truly of it is to attribute to it the being that defines it (agreement between thought and the thing's essence, which in turn agrees with the eternal divine thought that founds it); it grounds goodness insofar as, having a certain quantity of perfection, it is desirable in that proportion. **Being, unity, truth, and goodness are convertible** — different angles on the same reality, not separate additions to it.

## The universe as hierarchy of participation

No creature fully realizes its own essence or definition — there is always a "margin" short of complete self-identity, which is why finite beings are perpetually in motion, striving to maintain and complete themselves ([[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]]'s potency/act distinction is Aquinas's formula for exactly this fact of experience). Since "the more or less can only be evaluated relative to a maximum, the relative only relative to the absolute" (echoing *S.Th.* I, 44, 1), beings arrange themselves in a hierarchy running from God (pure and simple being) to sheer nothingness: **angels** (*prope Deus*, pure intelligences, near-nothing of matter), **material forms** (*prope nihil*), and **man** as the horizon/frontier between spirit and body, closing the gap between angel and matter as angel closes the gap between man and God. Each degree of being has a corresponding degree/mode of operation (an entity acts according to its degree of actuality).

## Analogy as what makes the hierarchy possible without pantheism

"Being" signifies two different modes of existence when applied to God and to creatures — so no question of "addition" or "subtraction" arises between them. Creaturely being is **only an analogue, an image, an imitation** of divine being: "as reflections kindle around a flame, multiply, fade and go out without affecting the flame's substance, so the likenesses freely created by the divine substance owe it everything they have of being, subsist only through it, and yet borrow nothing from a mode of being that is not theirs, add nothing to it, and take nothing from it." This **analogy of being** is what lets Aquinas maintain a real distinction between Creator and creature while still linking them in a single explanatory order — physics of things must subordinate itself to a metaphysics of essences, because if creatures are, in their radical origin, likenesses, analogy must explain the structure of the universe just as it explains creation itself.

## Against reading Thomism as Neo-Platonism

Gilson explicitly rejects the thesis (common enough to need refuting) that this hierarchical, participatory, Pseudo-Dionysian-flavored cosmos makes Aquinas a Plotinian. His argument: the Pseudo-Dionysius supplies only the **framework** of hierarchy — the necessity of thinking the universe hierarchically — not its **content**, and not the principle governing the order and distribution of its degrees; that is entirely Aquinas's own work. More decisively: Aquinas's God is **Augustine's** personal, willing, freely-choosing Creator (Jehovah) — not Plotinus's impersonal, necessarily-emanating One. "Between the freely created universe and the creating God there is an unbridgeable abyss, and no continuity but that of order. Properly speaking, **the world is an ordered discontinuity** (*une discontinuité ordonnée*)." This is, in Gilson's words, "the antipodes of Neo-Platonic philosophy." The same divergence holds for the doctrine of soul and knowledge: Aquinas's man is **Aristotle's** man (soul dependent on sense knowledge, no direct intuition of the intelligible, no reminiscence or ecstatic reunion with the One), not Plotinus's/Plato's.

## Garrigou-Lagrange's parallel account: formaliter eminenter

[[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Seconda, Cap. III ("L'eminenza della Deità," *S.Th.* I qq.12-13) works the same analogy doctrine from a different angle — not the hierarchy of creatures but the unity of God's own attributes. The Deity as it is in itself (not merely as *ipsum esse subsistens*, which contains the divine attributes only *actu implicite*) is naturally unknowable by any created or creatable intellect — this is an absolute metaphysical impossibility grounded in divine transcendence (against **Scotus**, for whom it is only contingent on a free divine decree, and against **Suárez**'s residual "active obediential potency"), not merely a *de facto* limit. Absolute perfections (being, truth, goodness, wisdom) are in God **formally** (*substantialiter*, not merely *virtualiter/causaliter* — "God is good" doesn't just mean he *causes* goodness, else "God is a body" would follow too) and **properly** (not metaphorically, unlike "God is angry"). But they are attributed to God and creatures **analogically**, not univocally (Scotus) nor equivocally (nominalists/Maimonides-style agnosticism) — Aquinas's own formula, quoted directly: "*omnis effectus non adaequans virtutem causae agentis, recipit similitudinem agentis... deficienter, ita ut quod divisim et multipliciter est in effectibus, in causa est simpliciter et eodem modo*" (an effect not equaling its cause's power receives the agent's likeness *deficiently*, so that what is divided and multiplied in the effects preexists unitedly and simply in the cause) — matching the Thomist definition of analogy (*ratio simpliciter diversa, secundum quid eadem*) against the Suarezian definition (*ratio simpliciter eadem, secundum quid diversa*).

The hardest question — how divine perfections (wisdom, mercy, justice) can be really identical in God *without destroying each other* — is answered via **Cajetan's** solution (accepted as the standard Thomist reading): wisdom and justice don't identify formally *with each other* (which would destroy both) but each is *formaliter eminenter* contained in the higher-order reality of the **Deity** itself, which is "super ens, super unum" — above being, above unity — and so can contain all perfections formally and eminently without any of them collapsing into synonymy. This is the same analogical, hierarchical structure Gilson describes for creatures, applied reflexively to the unity-in-plurality of God's own attributes and (by extension) the divine Persons.
