# Teologia Morale nella Sintesi Tomistica

Aquinas's moral theology per [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Settima (9 chapters, read in full) — the *Prima Secundae*'s general moral theology (last end, human acts, habits/virtues/vices, law, grace) and the *Secunda Secundae*'s special moral theology (theological/cardinal virtues, perfection, charisms).

## Last end and beatitude

Every rational agent acts for an end, and infinite regress in subordination of ends (as of efficient causes) is impossible — there must be a **last end** desired above all else, at least confusedly (everyone desires happiness, though many don't realize true happiness is in God). Wealth, honors, glory, power, bodily goods, pleasure, science, and acquired virtue **cannot** constitute it: the will's object is the *universal* good (as the intellect's is the universal true), and no finite created good — only participated goodness — can satisfy an appetite of unlimited, universal scope. This grounds a **proof of God's existence from desire**: a natural desire founded not on imagination or confused reasoning but on the very nature and universal amplitude of the will cannot be in vain or chimerical; therefore an unlimited, pure Good really existing (not merely conceived) must exist to correspond to it. Had man been created in a purely natural state, true happiness would have consisted in natural knowledge/love of God as author of nature; but revelation shows we are gratuitously called to **essentially supernatural beatitude** — the immediate vision of the divine essence, formally constituting beatitude, with love, enjoyment, bodily glorification, and the communion of saints as its necessary complement.

## Human acts: psychology, morality, conscience

**Psychological structure**: acts *elicited* by the will regarding the end (simple willing, efficacious intention, enjoyment) and regarding means (consent, choice/*electio*), each directed by a corresponding intellectual act (simple apprehension of the good, judgment, counsel, the last practical judgment) — culminating in *imperium* (command) and *usus* (active use of the faculties). **Moral structure**: acts are specified essentially by their **object**, but goodness/malice also depend on the **end** and **circumstances** — the same act can have a double goodness or malice (almsgiving done for vainglory). No deliberate concrete act is indifferent as to end, though some are indifferent as to object alone (walking). Notably, not every human act is *deliberated* in the strict discursive sense — acts moved by the gifts of the Holy Spirit (e.g. the gift of counsel) are free and meritorious without discursive deliberation, proceeding from *operating* rather than *cooperating* grace.

**Conscience and probabilism** — a historically significant methodological note: Aquinas defines opinion as an intellectual act inclining to one side of a contradiction "with fear of the other" (*cum formidine alterius*), meaning that when one side is *certainly* more probable, the other is not probably true but probably false, and it is unreasonable to act against the more probable side. In 1577 the Dominican **Bartolomé de Medina** proposed instead that a merely probable opinion may licitly be followed *even when the opposite is more probable* — the origin of **probabilism**, adopted by many Jesuits, that Garrigou-Lagrange judges a departure from Aquinas's own philosophical sense of "probable." Pascal's *Provincial Letters* (1656) publicized the resulting abuses; Alexander VII pressed the Dominican order to combat probabilist doctrines that same year, and (per Mandonnet) no Dominican writer defended probabilism thereafter. Garrigou-Lagrange favors instead the Gardeil/Beaudouin/Deman line reconciling Aquinas's principles with St. Alphonsus Liguori's *equiprobabilism*, grounded in prudential certainty "*per conformitatem ad appetitum rectum*" (conformity to right intention) rather than a reflex principle detached from virtue.

## Virtues, habits, vices, sin

**Habitus** (imperfectly translated "habit"): operative qualities, acquired or infused, specified by their **formal object** — the single principle ("*habitus ut dispositiones ordinatae ad operationem, specie distinguuntur secundum obiecta specie differentia*") that Antonino Reginaldo ranked as one of Thomism's three foundational truths (alongside *ens est transcendens/analogum* and *Deus est actus purus* — see [[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]] and [[Le Cinque Vie]]). **Against Scotist/nominalist-influenced readings** that would let infused and acquired virtues share the same (naturally accessible) formal object, differing only in their productive principle: Thomists (with Suárez, here, in agreement) insist an essentially supernatural object cannot be reached except by an essentially supernatural act — otherwise infused faith would be "useless, or useful only *ad facilius credendum*," approaching semi-Pelagianism.

**Three kinds of virtue**: intellectual (understanding of first principles, science, wisdom, prudence, art), moral (the four cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance — directing will and sensitive appetite), and theological (faith, hope, charity — elevating intellect/will to proportion with the supernatural end). **Acquired vs. infused moral virtues are specifically distinct** (not just in productive principle) by formal object, rule, and end — "an interval like an octave between two notes of the same name": acquired temperance keeps the mean for living rationally and healthily; infused temperance keeps a higher mean for living as a child of God journeying toward eternity, requiring severer mortification (1 Cor 9:27). The seven gifts of the Holy Spirit render the soul docile to the Spirit's inspirations "as sails dispose a boat to the favorable wind," growing together with charity like five fingers of a hand.

**Sin**: analogically (not univocally) divided into mortal (*aversio a fine ultimo*, properly *contra legem*, of itself irreparable) and venial (a disorder about means, *praeter legem*, reparable) — paralleling the essence/esse, act/potency analogical structure documented throughout this vault. The seven capital sins (per St. Gregory: vainglory, envy, wrath, avarice, sloth, gluttony, lust) derive from disordered self-love, concupiscence of flesh/eyes, and pride of life. Original sin (already documented in [[Angeli e Anima Umana (Sintesi Tomistica)]]) is formally the privation of original justice, materially concupiscence.

## Law

Defined as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, promulgated by one who has care of the community." Descends from **eternal law** (divine wisdom directing all acts and motions) through **natural law** (its rational-creature participation, immutable as human nature itself, first precept: do good, avoid evil) to **positive divine and human law**. Aquinas's insistence on the **immutability of natural law** is contrasted sharply with **Scotus** (only precepts concerning God directly are necessary; if God revoked "thou shalt not kill," killing an innocent would not be sinful) and **Ockham** (God, infinitely free, could have commanded us to hate him) — Leibniz's verdict on Ockham's view: this "dishonors God," making him as much the evil principle of the Manicheans as the good principle of the orthodox. Aquinas holds firmly against this nominalist trajectory toward complete legal positivism (echoed later in Gerson) that natural right, like human nature, is immutable and must illuminate all legislation worthy of the name.

## The treatise on grace

The most extensively documented chapter, directly extending [[Grazia Efficace e Premozione Fisica]] with material not covered there:
- **Necessity**: fallen man, with only God's natural concourse, cannot know the whole body of natural truths, nor believe supernatural truths on their proper (supernatural) motive, without grace — against Scotus/nominalists/Molina, who hold the assent of faith is natural in substance and supernatural only "by an added modality" (a "supernatural applied," violating the object-specifies-the-act principle). Fallen man, without **healing grace** (*gratia sanans*), cannot even love God above all with an *effectively* efficacious love — Thomists hold fallen man has *less* natural strength to keep the moral law than he would have had in a state of pure nature (against Molina), since his will is now turned away not just indirectly but the corruption itself constitutes a further defect.
- **Essence**: sanctifying grace is not a mere extrinsic denomination (as when a poor child is "adopted" by a rich man) but something **real** in the soul — God's love, unlike human love, does not presuppose but **produces** the beloved's loveliness ("*amor Dei est infundens et creans bonitatem in rebus*"). This directly refutes Luther's doctrine of justification by mere extrinsic imputation. Grace is a **permanent supernatural quality**, "a second nature," and — the vault's recurring theme of participation in the Deity as such, not merely in *ipsum esse subsistens* (see [[Gerarchia degli Esseri e Analogia]]) — a **formal, physical (though analogical) participation in the divine nature**: "the smallest degree of sanctifying grace is worth more than the good of the whole natural universe" (*bonum gratiae unius maius est quam bonum naturae totius universi*).
- **Divisions**: sanctifying (habitual) vs. actual grace; operating vs. cooperating actual grace (the latter moves the will via an antecedent act, the former via unforeseen special inspiration); *gratiae gratis datae*/charisms (external signs like prophecy, inferior to sanctifying grace, obtainable even in mortal sin) vs. sanctifying grace itself.
- **Sufficient vs. efficacious grace**: repeated and extended here beyond [[Grazia Efficace e Premozione Fisica]] with the full scriptural grounding (Prov 1:24, Isa, Matt 23:37, Acts 7:51 for resistible sufficient grace; Ezek 36:26-27, Sirach 33:13, John 10:27-28, Phil 2:13, and the Second Council of Orange against semi-Pelagianism for intrinsically efficacious grace) and Bossuet's formula: two graces "one [sufficient] leaves the will without excuse before God, the other [efficacious] does not let it glory in itself."
- **Obediential potency**: the soul's aptitude to receive whatever God wills to give it, limited only by contradiction — grace is drawn from this potency, which is *formally passive* (though *materially* active when rooted in an active faculty like the will receiving infused charity) — explicitly distinguished from the Scotist and Suarezian *active* obediential potency (already documented in [[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]] and [[Incarnazione, Unione Ipostatica e Mariologia]]).
- **Justification**: real remission of sins (against Luther's mere non-imputation) via infusion of habitual grace — Thomists hold, against Scotus and Suárez, that **not even by absolute divine power** can mortal sin and sanctifying grace coexist in the same subject (a contradiction in terms, like being simultaneously just and unjust).
- **Merit**: an analogical (not univocal) concept with four senses — Christ's strict-justice merit (infinite, intrinsic, independent of divine acceptance — against Scotus), the just person's *de condigno* merit (a real but promise-dependent right to reward), *de congruo proprie* merit (grounded in the "*ius amicabile*" of charity — how one person can merit *for another*, e.g. Monica for Augustine, Mary for all humanity), and *de congruo late dictum* (grounded only in God's liberality, without presupposing grace). Six conditions for *de condigno* merit; crucially, **the principle of merit cannot itself be merited** — so no one can merit the first grace, nor (per Trent, sess. VI cap. 13) the grace of final perseverance itself, which can only be *obtained* through humble, persevering prayer, never strictly merited.

## Theological virtues

Each is analyzed by **formal motive** — the recurring principle that a theological virtue's formal motive must be **uncreated**, i.e. God himself, never a creature however noble:
- **Faith**: formal object is *veritas prima* (God's own truthfulness/infallibility as revealer), not the Church's proposition or miracles (which are only the *sine qua non* condition). Against Scotus/nominalists/Molina (who hold faith's formal object is accessible to acquired/natural faith, given miracles as external confirmation) — for Thomists this would make infused faith unnecessary or merely useful "*ad facilius credendum*," the Pelagian position condemned by Orange II. One single act "*credimus Deo revelanti et Deum revelatum*."
- **Hope**: formal motive is *Deus auxilians* — not one's own effort or any created help, but God's goodness, mercy, fidelity, and omnipotence considered precisely as *ready to help*. Against the Quietists (who saw hope's self-referential structure, "I desire God for myself," as inherently disordered next to charity's pure disinterestedness): Cajetan's formula, "*desidero Deum mihi, non propter me, sed finaliter propter Deum*" — I desire God for myself, but the *final* reason for that desire is God himself, not me — resolves the apparent tension, crucial for interpreting St. John of the Cross's "passive purification" as intensifying rather than sacrificing hope.
- **Charity**: a real, created, supernatural *friendship* (not mere benevolence) with God, grounded in the *convictus* only grace provides — the same "friendship structure" already documented for attrition/contrition in [[I Sacramenti nella Sintesi Tomistica]]. Formal motive: divine goodness known supernaturally, loved for itself above all things and all God's gifts. One single virtue for love of God and neighbor (God as principal object, neighbor/self as secondary, "*ut in Deo sit*"). Charity is "the form" (extrinsically) of all other virtues, commanding and ordering their acts to the last end and rendering them meritorious; without it, other virtues are not "*in statu perfectae virtutis*."

## Moral (cardinal) virtues

**Prudence** (*auriga virtutum*, "*recta ratio agibilium*") directs all others via counsel, judgment, and command; prudential truth is measured "*per conformitatem ad appetitum rectum*" — a speculatively false judgment reached through blameless ignorance can be practically true. **Justice** (in the will, ordering us "*ad alterum*") divides into commutative, distributive, legal/social, and *epicheia* (equity, attending to the legislator's spirit over the letter — "*summum ius, summa iniuria*"); distinguished sharply from charity, which gives *beyond* what is owed. **Fortitude**'s principal act is *sustinere* (endurance), harder and more meritorious than *aggredi* (attack) — grounding the analysis of true vs. false martyrdom via the connection of virtues (a true martyr shows charity, prudence, humility alongside fortitude). **Temperance** governs the concupiscible appetite; **consecrated virginity** is a virtue distinct from and superior to chastity (as magnificence is to simple liberality), disposing to contemplation. **Humility**, annexed to temperance, is analyzed via Anselm's graded scale and shown to be Adam's and the devil's sin in reverse — pride specifically in desiring a knowledge/autonomy not yet possessed (unlike the angel, who already possessed perfect knowledge).

## Christian perfection and the states of life

Perfection consists **essentially in charity** (not in faith/hope, which can coexist with mortal sin, nor in the moral virtues, which don't unite us directly to God) — achievable within marriage via the *spirit* of the evangelical counsels even without their literal practice, though the counsels remain a faster, surer route. Grounds the striking Thomistic thesis that **love of God is, in this life, superior to knowledge of God** ("*melior est amor Dei quam cognitio*") — because knowledge draws God down to the limits of our finite ideas, while love elevates us toward him as he is in himself, hidden dimensions included; a saint's act of love (Aquinas's example: the Curé d'Ars teaching catechism) outweighs a sophisticated theological meditation inspired by lesser love. Perfection falls under the **supreme precept** of total love (Luke 10:27) not as something to be *immediately* accomplished but as an unlimited end all must tend toward, each according to their state (marriage, religious life, priesthood/episcopacy) — against any reading (which Garrigou-Lagrange associates partly with Suárez) that would cap the precept's scope and relegate its fullness to mere "counsel." Three kinds of life: contemplative (more noble, "the better part," eternal), active (dispensing to contemplation via ordered passions), and mixed/apostolic (most perfect of all — "more perfect to illuminate others than to illuminate oneself alone" — flowing from the fullness of contemplation, as with the Apostles after Pentecost and bishops as their successors).

## Charisms

*Gratiae gratis datae* (prophecy, miracles, discernment of spirits) given for others' benefit, inferior to sanctifying grace. Aquinas's treatment of **prophetic revelation** (requiring supernatural proposition of a hidden truth + infused light to judge its truth and divine origin infallibly) and especially of **biblical inspiration** are singled out by Garrigou-Lagrange as of towering importance — quoting even the non-Thomist Fr. Pesch's admission that "St. Thomas so polished the doctrine of Scriptural inspiration that for many centuries scarcely anything of moment was added to it," a doctrine *Providentissimus Deus* (the "magna charta" of biblical studies) promulgated authoritatively. Aquinas distinguishes **revelation** (new content, *acceptio specierum*) from **inspiration** (a divine *judgment* on already-acquired knowledge — the Evangelists already knew the facts they narrated; inspiration renders their *judgment* about those facts divinely certain) via the principal-cause/instrumental-cause framework already documented for the Eucharist and Christ's humanity: "*auctor principalis sacrae Scripturae est Spiritus Sanctus... homo fuit auctor instrumentalis*" — a genuinely instrumental, not dictated, authorship (extending *usque ad verba*, to the very words, but subordinated to and secondary relative to the truth to be expressed) that preserves the human author's own style and literary genre.
