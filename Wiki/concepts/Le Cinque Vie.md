# Le Cinque Vie

Aquinas's five proofs of God's existence, per Garrigou-Lagrange's systematic treatment in [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Seconda, Cap. II (cross-references [[god-philosophy-universities]] ch. 9's independent treatment of the third way, and MacIntyre's citation of *S.Th.* Ia, 2.3).

## Not provable a priori

God's existence cannot be proven a priori, even though "*Deus est*" is self-evident *in itself* (*per se nota quoad se*) to one who knows what God is. We do not know *a priori* what God is (*nescimus de Deo quid est*) — we start only with a confused nominal definition ("first cause of the world"), from which concrete existence cannot be deduced. We must argue *a posteriori*, from the contingent realities of experience, asking whether they require a really-existing first cause corresponding to that nominal definition. This is **moderate realism**, positioned between nominalism (which leads to agnosticism, e.g. Hume) and exaggerated realism (Parmenides, Plato, Neoplatonists, and — differently — Anselm's ontological argument, Spinoza, Malebranche, the ontologists, who claim an immediate confused intuition of God's nature rather than an abstract idea).

## The common foundation: the "proper cause" argument

All five ways rest on the principle of causality in its deepest formulation: what participates in existence (has *limited* existence) ultimately depends on a cause that must *be* Existence itself. Critically, the cause required is **not found in the series of past causes** (a father and grandfather, often no longer existing, were themselves contingent and needed a cause too — no being in a past causal chain can say "I am life/existence itself"). Nor does it matter whether the past series is infinite — even an eternally-existing world of contingent beings would still depend, at every instant, on a supreme cause, exactly as a footprint eternally imprinted in sand would still depend causally (though not temporally) on the foot. The cause required is found instead in the series of **essentially/necessarily subordinated causes actually and presently existing** — the "proper cause" (*causa propria*) that is immediately and necessarily required by its proper effect, the way a circle's properties necessarily presuppose the circle's nature. This series (unlike an accidentally-subordinated series stretching into the past) cannot regress infinitely without eliminating the effect altogether — "if there were no supreme mover, there would be no cause of motion, and motion... would not exist."

## The five ways in outline

1. **From motion**: motion, having no ground of its own being (*ratio sui*) — whether bodily or the spiritual "motion" of intellect/will — requires a First Mover (of both bodies and spirits).
2. **From efficient causes**: presently-existing, essentially-subordinated efficient causes (e.g. what currently sustains our life — atmospheric pressure, heat) require a Supreme Cause able to give causality and life to the others and conserve them.
3. **From contingency**: contingent beings (which can not-exist) require a Necessary Being having existence through itself, able to give it to others — if nothing existed even for an instant, nothing would ever exist, and if only contingent beings existed, their existence would be groundless.
4. **From degrees of perfection**: beings of greater/lesser perfection, truth, goodness participate, in varying measure, in existence/nobility/truth/goodness they only partly possess — composition (of participating subject + participated perfection) presupposes the simple, as union presupposes unity ("*quae enim secundum se diversa sunt, non conveniunt in aliquod unum nisi per aliquam causam adunantem ipsa*"). There must be One who can say not just "I have existence, truth, life" but "I *am* Being, Truth, Life."
5. **From finality/governance**: the natural tendency of bodies, plants, animals, and humans toward a fitting good/end (eye for seeing, wing for flying, intellect for truth, will for the good) requires a supreme Ordaining Intelligence — which must itself *be* Wisdom/Truth, not merely ordered toward them (else it too would need an orderer).

## The Kantian objection and its rebuttal

Kant objected that the unintelligibility of an uncaused contingent being is unintelligible *only for us*, given our intellect's constitution, but perhaps not absurd in itself. Garrigou-Lagrange's reply: the absurd just *is* what repels existence because it lies outside intelligible being (the intellect's object) with no possible relation to it — this is exactly the case of a "relation of fittingness between two terms that do not fit at all." The denial of causality is not as immediately self-contradictory as denying the definition of the contingent, but it is a **mediate** contradiction — denying the contingent's *immediate property* (Aristotle's "first mode of *per se*"), which, examined closely, is a true contradiction: it affirms the "uncaused union of the diverse" and a positive relation of fittingness (existence "suiting" a subject) between terms that have nothing positive in common by which they could fit.

## The keystone all five converge on

All five ways terminate at the same keystone: **Being itself subsisting** (*ipsum esse subsistens*). Each way's terminus (first mover, first cause, necessary being, supreme being, supreme ordaining intelligence) can belong only to one whose mode of *acting* follows a mode of *being* that simply *is* existence — not one who merely *has* existence, but who *is* the Existence, Truth, and Life themselves. This is the same *ipsum esse subsistens* doctrine documented in [[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]] and [[god-philosophy-universities]], reached here via the five ways rather than via the essence/esse composition argument directly — the two are, for Garrigou-Lagrange, two routes (*via inventionis* ascending, *via iudicii* descending) to the same summit: **only in God are essence and existence identical**.

## Verified against the primary text (2026-07-26)

Read directly at *S.Th.* I, q.2, a.3 (see [[somma-teologica-de-deo-uno]]): Aquinas gives all five ways within this single article, immediately after answering two objections (the problem of evil; causal-parsimony). No divergence from the account above — Garrigou-Lagrange's synthesis tracks the primary text closely, including the terse, formulaic close of each way ("...e tutti riconoscono che esso è Dio").

## Compared against the Contra Gentiles (2026-07-26)

*Contra Gentiles* I, ch.13 — see [[contra-gentiles-libro-primo]] — gives Aristotle's own proofs at far greater length than the Summa's First Way: an entire chapter (two arguments from motion, each with premise-by-premise proof and explicit handling of the eternity-of-the-world and animated-sphere objections) devoted to what the Summa compresses into one clause. The *Contra Gentiles* passage covers only the motion-based arguments in this chapter; the other four ways' Contra Gentiles parallels appear in later, not-yet-mapped chapters of Libro I.
