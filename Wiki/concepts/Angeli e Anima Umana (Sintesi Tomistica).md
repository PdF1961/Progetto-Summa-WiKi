# Angeli e Anima Umana

Aquinas's treatises on angels and man, per [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Quarta (10 chapters, read in full). Throughout, the recurring pattern is Aquinas charting a middle course between **Scotus** and (in eclectic eclipse) **Suárez**, both criticized for insufficiently respecting the principle that *faculties, habits, and acts are specified by their formal object* — the single principle Antonino Reginaldo's 17th-century *Doctrinae D. Thomae tria principia* ranks alongside "*ens est transcendens seu analogum*" and "*Deus est actus purus*" as one of Thomism's three foundational truths.

## Angels: pure spirits, individuated without matter

Angels are **purely spiritual, subsistent forms without any matter** (against **Scotus**, who holds them composed of form + "incorporeal matter without quantity," and **Suárez**, who agrees with Aquinas on pure spirituality but sides with Scotus's conclusion that many angels can share one species). Consequence: **no two angels can share the same species** — since individuation of a specific form requires matter (as two drops of water are distinguished only by the matter receiving an identical form), a form *not* received in matter is necessarily unique: "*forma irrecepta in materia est unica*" — the same principle that proves God's uniqueness (*ipsum esse irreceptum est subsistens et unicum*).

**Angelic knowledge** is proportioned to its proper object: the intelligible being of *spiritual* creatures (as human knowledge's proper object is the intelligible being of *sensible* things, and God's own is the divine essence itself) — three cleanly distinct orders of intellectual life. Consequently angelic ideas are **innate/infused** (not abstracted from sense data), simultaneously **universal and concrete** (representing a whole species and all its individual members at once), and knowledge is **purely intuitive, never discursive** — angels see conclusions in principles and means in ends immediately, without composing/dividing judgments the way humans do. Higher angels need fewer, more universal ideas. Scotus (denying that intellects are specified by proper objects, and holding that immediate vision of the divine essence would be *naturally* available to angels or humans if God had simply so decreed) allows angels to reason discursively and even err about creatures' natures — for Thomists a demotion of pure spirit toward human-style cognition.

**Angelic will**: loves the good it grasps as perfect (its own natural being, God as author of its nature) **necessarily** (not freely) — against Scotus/Suárez, who deny any necessary act in angelic will at all. Because angelic cognition is simultaneous/intuitive rather than successive, angelic choice, once made with full deliberation, is **irrevocable** — this is Aquinas's explanation of why the devil's fall is unpardonable and permanent (he considered everything at once; there is no "you didn't think of this" left to reconsider), and equally why the good angels' choice is fixed forever. Governing principle throughout: "*nihil praevolitum nisi praecognitum ut convenientius hic et nunc*" (nothing is willed beforehand except as foreknown as more fitting here and now) — will cannot act without intellectual direction, against Scotus/Suárez's stronger voluntarism.

**State before/after the "trial"**: all three schools (Aquinas, Scotus, Suárez) agree angels received sanctifying grace at the instant of creation and were confirmed (good) or fixed in obstinacy (fallen) after a single free act — but Aquinas alone holds angels received their **full natural perfection and natural beatitude instantly** (given non-discursive cognition) and that Christ's merits did **not** earn the angels' essential grace/glory (he is Redeemer of men, not angels) — against Scotus (who holds Christ would have incarnated even without Adam's sin, and merited angels' grace too) and Suárez (similar, minus the Passion).

## Man: soul as sole substantial form, spiritual yet the body's form

Against **Averroes/Siger of Brabant** (a single, eternal, impersonal separate intellect temporarily "illuminating" individual bodies — denying personal immortality and freedom) and against **pre-Thomist Augustinian theologians** (Alexander of Hales, Bonaventure: plurality of substantial forms + "spiritual matter" in the soul, compromising the body-soul composite's natural unity), Aquinas holds: the rational soul is **purely spiritual** (hence incorruptible) yet **the body's sole substantial form** — intrinsically independent of matter in its intellective/volitive operations and its very being, while still constituting, with the body, one single nature (*aliquid unum per se*), not an accidental union (which would make Socrates not truly "one," not truly *a being* at all — "*sequitur quod Socrates non sit unum simpliciter, et per consequens nec ens simpliciter*").

**Proof of spirituality/immortality**: operation follows being, and intellectual activity is demonstrably independent of matter — proven by the three degrees of abstraction (physical science abstracts from individual matter; mathematics abstracts from all sensible qualities to grasp quantity as such; metaphysics abstracts from all matter to grasp intelligible being itself, accessible only to intellect, never to imagination — the ground of the five ways and of the first principles of contradiction/causality/finality). Because our intellect grasps being absolutely and beyond all time-limitation, the soul **naturally desires to exist always**, and a natural desire grounded in nature itself cannot be in vain ("*omnis intellectualis substantia est incorruptibilis*"). Corresponds to **Theses XV and XVIII of the XXIV Tesi Tomiste**.

**Soul as sole substantial form** (**Thesis XVI**): the soul gives the body *all* its essential grades of perfection (corporeity, vegetative life, sensitive life, intellective life) and communicates to it its own single act of existing — "*ex actu et actu non fit unum per se in natura*" (two acts cannot make one *per se* being) but "*ex potentia essentialiter ordinata ad actum et ex actu potest fieri aliquid per se unum*" (potency essentially ordered to act, plus act, can make one *per se* being — as matter and form do). Suárez, understanding these act/potency principles differently, treats "the soul is the body's only form" as merely **probable**, not demonstrated — "the most frequent mark of his eclecticism," per Garrigou-Lagrange.

## Faculties: really distinct from the soul and from each other

Since only in God do essence, existence, intellect, will, and act of understanding/loving all coincide without real distinction, even in the angel — and *a fortiori* in the human soul — essence and existence, and essence and faculties, are **really distinct**. Scotus's alternative ("formal-actual distinction *ex natura rei*") is, for Thomists, an impossible hybrid between real and rational distinction (a distinction either precedes mental consideration, in which case it is already real however slight, or it doesn't, in which case it's merely rational — no third option). Suárez again takes a middle position: the real distinction between soul and faculties is *possible* but not certain.

From faculties' specification by formal object follows the **infinite qualitative gap between intellect and the senses** (sense perceives only the sensible/imaginable; only intellect grasps intelligible being, the first principles, and the first principle of morality) and between **will (rational appetite)** and **sensitive appetite** (concupiscible/irascible) — will is specified by the universal good only intellect can grasp, sensitive appetite by the sensible/pleasant/useful good alone, so sensitive appetite *as such* cannot will the rational/honest good that is virtue's object. Garrigou-Lagrange notes this "immense distance, *sine mensura*" between will and sensibility is denied by many modern psychologists after Rousseau.

**Free will, against Molina**: Molina's definition ("that agent is called free which, all requisites for acting having been posited, can act or not act") is, for Thomists, defective precisely because it abstracts from the object specifying the free act — and is tied to *scientia media* (see [[Grazia Efficace e Premozione Fisica]]). The Thomist definition instead: "*libertas est indifferentia dominatrix voluntatis erga obiectum a ratione propositum ut non ex omni parte bonum*" — freedom is the will's *dominating indifference* toward any object reason proposes as good under one aspect but not another; freedom arises from the disproportion between a will specified by the universal good and any particular, partial good. Corresponds to **Thesis XXI**: choice follows the last practical judgment, but it is the choice itself, by accepting that judgment's direction, that makes it "last" — "a kind of marriage" between intellect and will, against Scotus/Suárez, who (per the Thomists) cannot explain how choice could occur *without* intellectual direction at all.

## The separated soul

Proven to subsist after death (any simple, immaterial form is intrinsically independent of matter in being, operation, and production). Against the Averroist worry that a separated soul, lacking matter (the principle of individuation), would collapse into one impersonal soul for all humanity: Aquinas holds the soul has an essential ("transcendental") relation to *its own* body, which persists even after that body's dissolution into dust — so Peter's separated soul remains really distinct from Paul's. The separated state is **preternatural, not the soul's most perfect state** (its proper object remains the being of sensible things, known through the senses) — hence the soul naturally desires reunion with its body (harmonizing with the resurrection dogma), though it cannot reunite itself by its own will (it informs the body through its nature, not through voluntary operations).

The separated soul **knows itself immediately** (its own spirituality, immortality, freedom, and natural law inscribed in its essence, and God as its nature's author — now known "in the mirror of its own essence" rather than of sensible things) — a point Garrigou-Lagrange stresses is too often left "in the shadow." Its **will is immutably fixed** on its last end, good or bad, at the moment of separation — because the body was given to the soul precisely to help it tend toward its end while still "on the way" (*in via*); once separated, it is no longer *in via* but rests (or is fixed) in whatever end its last meritorious/demeritorious act, made while still embodied, secured.

## Original justice and original sin

A point on which **Aquinas's own view developed** across his career (traced through *Commento alle Sentenze* ~1254 → *De malo* 1263-68 → *Summa Theologica*): early on he merely reports, without deciding, the older view (Peter Lombard, Alexander of Hales, Albert, Bonaventure) that Adam was created only in "pure nature" and later disposed himself to receive grace. By the *De malo* he states plainly: "*originalis iustitia includit gratiam gratum facientem, nec credo verum esse, quod homo sit creatus in naturalibus puris*" — original justice **includes** sanctifying grace; he does not believe man was created in "pure nature." The *Summa*'s mature position: Adam was created **in a state of grace**, which grounded the "original rectitude" (mind's supernatural subjection to God) from which followed the subordination of passions to reason and of body to soul (with impassibility and immortality as further privileges). This claim later aligns closely with Trent's *sess. V, can. 2* (condemning the view that Adam's sin harmed only himself, not his progeny, regarding the "*sanctitatem et iustitiam*" received from God) — read by many Council Fathers as meaning sanctifying grace specifically.

Consequently: **original sin is a sin of *nature*** (voluntary only through Adam's will, not ours), formally constituted by the **privation of original justice** (of which grace is the primary element) — which is why Baptism, restoring grace, actually remits it (if original justice were merely natural integrity, Baptism, which doesn't restore that integrity, couldn't remit the sin). Its transmission is explained by the same soul-as-sole-substantial-form doctrine: though the soul is created *ex nihilo* directly by God (not generated), it constitutes, together with the generated body, **one single human nature** — "human nature is transmitted from parent to child through the transmission of flesh, to which the soul is subsequently infused, and from this it contracts the infection, because it becomes with the transmitted flesh one nature. If it were not united to it so as to constitute [one] nature, as an angel is united to an assumed body, it would not receive the infection" (quoted directly).

## Verified against the primary text (2026-07-26)

*Contra Gentiles* II, chs.56-59, 79 — read directly, see [[contra-gentiles-libro-secondo]] — give Aquinas's fullest primary-text treatment of the soul-as-substantial-form doctrine and its main rival theories: Plato's "pilot in a ship" model (refuted at ch.57 for reducing man to a being *per accidens*) and Averroes's shared-separate-intellect model (refuted at length in ch.59, absent from the Summa Teologica's more compressed treatment). The immortality argument at ch.79 confirms the "soul perfected by abstraction from bodily reality, not destroyed by separation from it" doctrine already summarized here via [[la-sintesi-tomistica]].
