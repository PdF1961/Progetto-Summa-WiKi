# Le XXIV Tesi Tomiste (1914)

Twenty-four theses summarizing the philosophical core of Thomism. **Origin, per [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Ottava Cap. I** (corrects the imprecise "1925" association): Card. Villeneuve's diagnosis of pre-1914 doctrinal chaos (everyone invoking Aquinas's authority for mutually contradictory positions) led Pius X, by motu proprio of **29 June 1914**, to require that *principia et pronuntiata maiora doctrinae Sancti Thomae* be taught in all courses of philosophy. Thomist professors proposed 24 fundamental theses to the Sacred Congregation of Studies, which examined and endorsed them. In February 1916 the Congregation made the *Summa Theologica* the set textbook and declared the theses "*proponantur veluti tutae normae directivae*" (to be proposed as safe directive norms — not imposed as matters of *de fide* assent). Benedict XV confirmed this on 7 March 1916. The 1917 Code of Canon Law, canon 1366 §2, codified the requirement that philosophy and theology be taught "*ad Angelici Doctoris rationem, doctrinam et principia*." Guido Mattiussi S.J. published the standard commentary, *Le XXIV Tesi della Filosofia di S. Tommaso*, in 1917. **1925** is a separate date: the year Garrigou-Lagrange presented a paper on the theses' applications at the International Thomistic Congress in Rome — later published as Part II of [[essenza-e-attualita-del-tomismo]], whose Appendice II reproduces the full Italian text of the theses. **Not** a statement of dogma (never entered the Denzinger collection) — a disciplinary measure against philosophical deviations infiltrating Catholic teaching.

## The theses (summary)

**I–IV — Being, act/potency, analogy** (see [[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]])
1. Potency and act divide being: everything is either pure act or is really composed of act and potency as first intrinsic principles.
2. Act is limited only by the potency that is capacity for perfection; where act is pure it is unlimited and unique, where finite/multiple it is really composed with potency.
3. God alone, as *ipsum esse*, is simplest and subsists uniquely; all else, participating in esse, has essence and esse as really distinct principles.
4. "Being" (*ens*) is said of God and creatures neither univocally nor equivocally but **analogically** (attribution and proportionality).

**V–XII — Creatures, substance, accidents, quantity**
5. Every creature is a real composition of subsisting subject and completing forms, really distinct from substance.
6. Besides absolute accidents there is relation (*ad aliquid*), often with real entitative ground.
7. Spiritual creatures are absolutely simple in essence but doubly composed: essence/esse, substance/accidents.
8. Corporeal creatures are composed of potency and act within essence itself: matter and form.
9. Neither matter nor form has independent esse; neither is produced or destroyed without the other.
10. Substance and quantity are not identical in bodies; quantity is a true accident distinct from substance.
11. Matter, as it extrinsicates in quantity, is the principle of individuation — impossible in pure spirits.
12. By quantity a body occupies one place.

**XIII–XXI — Soul, cognition, will** (see also individuation, forms of life)
13. Living beings need organic structure (heterogeneous parts) since the soul, as substantial form, requires movers and moved parts in the same subject.
14. Vegetative/sensitive souls have no proper subsistence; they cease with the body's corruption.
15. The human soul, by contrast, is per se subsistent, created by God, infused, and by nature incorruptible/immortal.
16. The rational soul is the body's **sole substantial form**, giving man all essential grades of perfection and communicating the act of existence to the body.
17. Faculties emanate from the soul in two orders — organic (sense, subject = composite) and inorganic (subject = soul alone); man has a faculty intrinsically independent of the organism.
18. Immateriality grounds intellectuality; the human intellect's proper object (in the present state of union) is quiddities abstracted from material conditions.
19. Knowledge derives from sensible things; since the sensible is not actually intelligible, an abstractive faculty is needed alongside the intellect that formally understands.
20. These species yield direct knowledge of universals; singulars are grasped via sense and via the intellect's cooperation with representations; spiritual realities are reached by analogy.
21. Will follows, does not precede, intellect; it necessarily tends to what intellect presents as the good, but chooses freely among the many goods proposed — the choice follows the last practical judgment, but that it is *last* depends on the will.

**XXII–XXIV — God's existence, essence, distinction from the world**
22. God's existence is known not by immediate intuition nor a priori, but strictly **a posteriori**, from effects to cause (the five ways in condensed form: motion → unmoved mover; causal subordination → uncaused first cause; contingency → necessary being; graded perfections → supreme exemplar; order of the universe → ordering separate intellect).
23. The divine essence, identical to its own act of existing (*ipsum esse*), is thereby metaphysically well-proposed to us, and this grounds the infinity of its perfections.
24. God is distinguished from all finite things by the purity of His esse. Consequence: the world can only derive from **creation**; no finite creature can be given creative power (which per se and primarily produces *ens qua ens*); no created agent can influence the esse of any effect without prior motion from the First Cause.

## Significance

Garrigou-Lagrange treats the theses as a condensed, magisterially-sanctioned map of the applications he derives at length in [[Dottrina di Atto e Potenza]] and as the doctrinal line separating strict Thomism from Suarezian eclecticism and Scotist univocity — see [[Tomismo]].
