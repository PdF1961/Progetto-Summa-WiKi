# I Sacramenti nella Sintesi Tomistica

Per [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Sesta (7 chapters, read in full).

## Sacraments in general

The seven sacraments are efficacious signs of grace, producing it **ex opere operato** through **physical instrumental causality** (not merely moral causality) — "*principalis autem causa efficiens gratiae est ipse Deus, ad quem comparatur humanitas Christi sicut instrumentum coniunctum, sacramentum autem sicut instrumentum separatum*" (God is the principal efficient cause; Christ's humanity is his *conjoined* instrument, the sacrament a *separated* instrument). All Thomists except Melchior Cano accept this "physical" (real, not merely moral) causality. Matter/form analysis applies analogically (words determine the significance of the sensible matter — e.g. baptismal formula + ablution; in Matrimony, consent alone contains both matter and form). The seven sacraments' number is shown fitting by analogy to natural life's needs (birth, growth, nourishment, healing → Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist, Penance, Extreme Unction) plus social functions (public office, propagation → Orders, Matrimony).

## Transubstantiation

Necessary specifically to explain Christ's **real presence** without moving his glorious, impassible body from heaven into every consecrated host: since that body cannot itself change or be "brought" (*adductio*) to a new place without contradicting its own impassibility, its becoming really present under the species of bread/wine can only occur through **the bread and wine's own substance changing into it** — "nothing can be somewhere it wasn't before except by local motion or by another thing being converted into it." **Against Scotus** (who explains real presence via annihilation of bread's substance + "adduction" of Christ's body from heaven — a view echoed partially by Bellarmine, de Lugo, Vázquez as "adductive transubstantiation") and **against Suárez** (transubstantiation as quasi-reproduction of Christ's body): the Thomist position, matching Trent's Catechism (drafted by Dominican theologians) verbatim, is that **the whole substance of bread is converted into the whole substance of Christ's body**, "*sine ulla Domini nostri mutatione*" — no change in Christ himself. Cajetan's formula for the terminus: "*id quod erat panis, iam est corpus Christi*." Explains, as corollaries (without multiplying causes — Aquinas's "principle of economy"): the accidents remaining *sine subiecto* (without any subject); Christ's quantity present *per modum substantiae* rather than *per modum quantitatis*; and his body being numerically identical in heaven and in every Eucharist, present "not as in a place, but after the manner of substance."

## The sacrifice of the Mass

Aquinas anticipated, in his own words, the exact objection later pressed by the Reformers (Zwingli: "*Christus semel tantum mactatus est... ergo semel tantum oblatus est*" — Christ was slain and his blood shed only once, therefore he was offered only once): if a true sacrifice requires **real immolation** of the victim, and Christ's body is now glorious and impassible, how is the Mass a true sacrifice and not mere commemoration? Aquinas's answer (*S.Th.* III q.83, a.1), later echoed by Trent: the Mass involves the Eucharistic celebration being called "Christ's immolation" in **two senses** — (1) as a **representative image** of the Passion (the true immolation), and (2) as making us **participants in the fruit** of that Passion. The double consecration (bread → Body, wine → Blood separately) constitutes a **real but sacramental (not physical/bloody) separation** of Body and Blood — a genuine, not merely commemorative, immolation, but *in genere signi* (in the order of sign), since even bloody sacrifices' external immolation was always itself a sign of interior oblation/contrition, without which it has no religious value (Cain's sacrifice). Garrigou-Lagrange judges this — sacramental immolation alone, no attempt to posit some additional "virtual" real immolation (a move some later Thomists like Gonet, Billuart, Hugon made under Suárez's influence, which Garrigou-Lagrange rejects as reviving, even if only "virtually," what Aquinas calls not a sacrifice but a *maleficium*) — the authentic Thomist and Tridentine position. **Christ himself is the principal priest** of every Mass, actually (not merely virtually, against Scotus/Amicus/de la Taille) willing each consecration — "*hoc sacramentum tantae est dignitatis quod non conficitur nisi in persona Christi*"; the celebrant says "*hoc est corpus meum*," not "this is Christ's body," speaking *in persona Christi*. The Mass's value is **infinite** (against Durand and Scotus), grounded in the infinite dignity of both victim and principal priest — one Mass benefits ten thousand as fully as one person, "as the sun equally warms a crowd or a single man."

## Attrition and contrition

Contrition proper proceeds from charity; **attrition** (imperfect contrition, in a soul still in mortal sin) raises a delicate question about its supernatural character. Against two opposed errors: **laxism** ("natural, honest attrition suffices" for justification when joined to absolution) and **Jansenism** (attrition lacking benevolent love of God for himself is not supernatural at all). Following Cajetan's *De contritione* and the Salmanticenses, Garrigou-Lagrange argues attrition, while not itself an act of charity (charity requires *convictus* — actual shared life via habitual grace, not just mutual benevolence — "there can be reciprocal benevolence between two men living far apart who know each other only by report, but between them there is not yet friendship"), does require an **initial love of benevolence toward God** as source of all justice — because one cannot detest an offense against God without already loving God as that offense's victim, just as one cannot detest a lie without already loving truth. This resolves the apparent contradiction (this initial benevolence, though real love of God, doesn't yet justify, since it lacks the *convictus* only habitual grace provides) and is grounded directly in Trent (*sess. VI, cap. 6*, on adult baptismal preparation: "*Deum tamquam omnis iustitiae fontem diligere incipiunt*").

## Revival of merits (reviviscentia meritorum)

A clear divergence between Aquinas and **Suárez** (followed by many moderns): Suárez holds *all* past merits, even those earned with great charity, fully "revive" at absolution regardless of how minimal the attrition — lose five talents of charity through mortal sin, regain exactly five upon absolution. **Aquinas holds otherwise**: grace, like any form admitting degrees, is received according to the subject's *actual disposition* — so the penitent rises with **more, equal, or less** grace than before, depending on the fervor of the present contrition/attrition, not automatically restored to the prior level (quoted directly from *S.Th.*: "*poenitens quandoque resurgit in maiori gratia... quandoque in aequali... quandoque in minori*"). Garrigou-Lagrange treats this as spiritually consequential, not merely academic: a soul that falls from an advanced state cannot resume its ascent from where it stumbled without a truly fervent contrition restoring the prior degree — otherwise it must resume from a markedly lower point. Cajetan and Billuart are read as preserving Aquinas's actual position against both Bañez (too restrictive) and Billot (too generous).

## The Church and the afterlife

Only briefly sketched (Aquinas's treatise on the Church was never systematized as such — later Thomists, from Torquemada's *Summa de Ecclesia* through Garrigou-Lagrange's own *De Revelatione per Ecclesiam catholicam proposita*, built it out): the Church as Christ's mystical body, possessing full infallible doctrinal authority extending even to "dogmatic facts," with civil power subordinate to spiritual power "as body to soul."

**Immutability of the separated soul** (*Contra Gentiles* c.91, 95, extensively quoted): revisits and reinforces the doctrine already documented in [[Angeli e Anima Umana (Sintesi Tomistica)]] — the soul's will regarding its last end becomes immutably fixed immediately after death, since the body (whose motion could indirectly alter the soul's dispositions) is no longer present to move the soul toward a goal *in via*; it now rests in the end already secured. **Cajetan's dissenting view** (that the separated soul's *first* act after separation is itself meritorious/demeritorious, collapsing the angel/human distinction) is reported as **generally rejected** by Thomists (Sylvester of Ferrara, the Salmanticenses), citing John 9:4 ("night comes, when no one can work") — merit/demerit belongs to the composite *man*, not the separated soul alone, so the last meritorious act must occur *before* death, not at the instant of separation.

## Verified against the primary text (2026-07-26)

*S.Th.* III, q.62 and q.75 — read directly, see [[somma-teologica-sacramenti]] — confirm the instrumental-causality doctrine (sacraments as causing, not merely signifying, grace, contrasted with the Old Law's sacraments per Gal 4:9) and the transubstantiation doctrine (q.75 a.3: Aquinas explicitly rejects both annihilation of the bread's substance and its resolution into pre-existing matter, in favor of direct substance-to-substance conversion). No divergence from Garrigou-Lagrange's synthesis found.
