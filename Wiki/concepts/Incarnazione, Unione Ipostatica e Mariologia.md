# Incarnazione, Unione Ipostatica e Mariologia

Aquinas's Christology and Mariology per [[la-sintesi-tomistica]] Parte Quinta (6 chapters, read in full).

## Motive of the Incarnation: the "if Adam had not sinned" debate

Aquinas holds that, **in the present providential plan**, if the first man had not sinned, the Word would not have become incarnate — the Incarnation's motive, per Scripture and Tradition ("*ubique ratio Incarnationis ex peccato primi hominis assignatur*"), is to offer God a satisfaction adequate to redeem humanity. Against **Scotus** (the Word would have incarnated regardless, to manifest divine goodness, but not "in passible flesh") and **Suárez** (redemption and manifestation of goodness are *coordinate* ends *ex aequo*, part of his general eclectic tendency to coordinate rather than subordinate). The Thomist counter-argument turns on **efficacious vs. conditional decrees** (see [[Grazia Efficace e Premozione Fisica]]): efficacious decrees, unlike conditional ones, extend from eternity to *all* circumstances of what is actually to be effected *hic et nunc* — and even Scotists concede the Incarnation "in passible flesh" presupposes Adam's sin, so the *actual* efficacious decree of the Incarnation as it occurred requires that presupposition. Resolved via Cajetan's distinction between **final causality** (God wills Christ, as manifestation of goodness, before Adam, considered as merely possible) and **material causality** (God permits Adam's sin, which must actually occur before the Incarnation-as-remedy is fitting) — as the soul is willed before the body in the order of finality, but the body must be willed/disposed first in the order of material causation for the soul to be infused. Conclusion, following Aquinas's *O felix culpa* remark (echoed in the Exsultet): the Incarnation's motive is **mercy**, and the good for which God permitted Adam's sin is retrospectively seen (*post factum*) to be the redemptive Incarnation itself and its universal radiance — while God's ultimate end remains the manifestation of his goodness, to which Christ is not subordinated (he is its *eminent cause*) but we are subordinated to Christ (1 Cor 3:22-23).

## Ontological personality and the hypostatic union

This chapter directly resolves the "ontological personality" question flagged as unread from [[Persona (Tomismo)]] (originally noted from *Essenza e attualità* / *Sintesi Tomistica* Parte Ottava). Aquinas's account of **what formally constitutes personhood** (adjudicated among rival Scholastic positions):
- **Scotus** (denying the real essence/existence distinction): personality is something *negative* — merely the *absence* of assumption by a divine Person (Peter and Paul are persons only because their individual human natures are *not* assumed as Christ's is).
- **Suárez**: personality is a substantial *mode*, posterior to a singular nature's existence.
- **Cajetan and the majority of Thomists** (the position Garrigou-Lagrange endorses as authentically Aquinas's): personality is **that by which a singular nature becomes immediately capable of receiving existence** (*id quo natura singularis fit immediate capax existentiae*) — positive and substantial, yet distinct from both the nature (which is attributed to the person as its essential part) and from existence (a contingent predicate of any creature, since "*nulla persona creata est suum esse*"). Personality is "like a point where two lines intersect" — the line of essence and the line of existence — "*id quo subiectum intelligens est id quod est*." This is why **"tres Personae non habent nisi unum esse"** in the Trinity (already documented in [[La Santissima Trinità (Sintesi Tomistica)]]): personality (three, incommunicable) differs from existence (one, communicable).

**Applied to Christ**: the human nature is united to the **Person** of the Word (not to the divine nature, which would collapse Chalcedon) — substantially, not accidentally, since human nature is not an accident like whiteness. Christ is truly man **without a human personality** of his own; the divine Person's union does not diminish but rather **glorifies** his humanity, giving it an innate, substantial, uncreated holiness. Individuation of Christ's human nature still comes from matter (born in a place, a time, among the Jewish people); his personality is uncreated. **No substantial "mode" mediates the union** (against Scotus, Suárez, Vázquez, and even some Thomists like the Carmelites of Salamanca and Godoy) — "between nature and *suppositum* nothing can fall as a medium, just as nothing falls between matter and form." The union is the most intimate of all created unions — more intimate even than soul-body union, since soul and body separate at death but the Word never separates from the assumed nature.

## Unum esse in Christo

The intimacy of the hypostatic union entails, for Aquinas and his school, **a single act of existence for the two natures** — presupposing the real essence/existence distinction. **Scotus and Suárez, denying that distinction, are committed to two substantial existences in Christ** — for Thomists this *compromises* the union, since existence (a subject's ultimate actuality) presupposes what formally constitutes the subject as such (its personality/subsistence); two substantial existences would mean two persons ("*impossibile est quod unius rei non sit unum esse*"). Christ's human nature is "terminated and possessed by the Word, who communicates to it his own existence, as he communicates his personality" — described as an "ecstasy of being," beyond even the "ecstasy of intelligence and love" given by the beatific vision.

## Consequences: sanctity, grace, priesthood, kingship, merit's infinite value

From the hypostatic union follow: Christ's **substantial, innate, uncreated holiness**; the **fullness of created habitual grace** (perfect from the first instant, not increased — Constantinople II — received *as head of humanity*, extensively as well as intensively, "*de plenitudine eius nos omnes accepimus*"); the beatific vision from the moment of conception (so his human intellect was simultaneously *viator et comprehensor*); his **priesthood** (many recent theologians hold it is constituted by the grace of union itself, not merely habitual grace); his right to **latria** (worship owed to God alone) even as man, and his **universal kingship/judgeship** as Man-God (the doctrinal basis of Pius XI's 1925 encyclical *Quas Primas*, Christ the King); and — the crux of the whole treatise on Redemption — the **intrinsically infinite value of Christ's meritorious/satisfactory acts**: for Thomists (against Scotus's view that the value is only *extrinsic*, by God's acceptance) the acts are infinite in value *intrinsically* because their *principium quod* (who properly acts, merits, satisfies) is the divine Person of the Word acting through the assumed humanity — "Christ, by suffering out of charity and obedience, exhibited to God something greater than the whole human race's offense would require as compensation" (quoted directly, echoing Clement VI).

## Freedom and impeccability reconciled

Christ's merit requires **true freedom** (*libertas a necessitate*, not mere spontaneity), yet he was **absolutely impeccable** — unable to sin, for three reasons: his divine personality (to which sin cannot be attributed), the beatific vision (from which the beatified soul cannot turn away), and grace received inamissibly as a consequence of the union. Resolution, refined especially in Bañez's era: (1) **freedom of exercise alone suffices** for true liberty — one need not be able to choose between contrary acts (love vs. hate), only to be able to do or not do a given act; (2) **the power to sin is not required for true freedom** — it is a form of the *defectibility* of created free will (like error is a defectibility of intellect), absent in God, in the confirmed blessed, and in Christ; (3) a distinction between **"disobeying" (privative — a fault)** and simply **"not obeying" (negative — mere absence of the act, as in one who sleeps)**: Christ could not disobey (even by omission), but could, in the merely negative sense, "not obey," because dying for us was not *hic et nunc* necessarily connected to his will or beatitude — it was a good mixed with a great non-good (immense suffering), which did not compel his will the way the *clare visa* divine goodness does. Summarized in Aquinas's own words: "*voluntas Christi, licet sit determinata ad bonum, non tamen est determinata ad hoc vel illud bonum*" — Christ chose, by free will confirmed in the good, exactly as the blessed do.

## The Passion

Three problems addressed: **(1)** How could beatific joy coexist with the Passion's suffering (the greatest possible in this life, given Christ's superior wisdom grasping the full gravity of sin and his immense love)? Answer: no "overflow" (*redundantia*) of glory/joy passed from the higher part of Christ's soul to the lower — he freely willed to abandon the lower faculties and sensibility to suffering without any mitigating radiance from the beatific vision at the summit, "like a great mountain whose peak is sunlit while its flanks are lashed by storm." **(2)** The Passion causes salvation in an ordered progression of five modes (*S.Th.* III q.48): merit, satisfaction, sacrifice, redemption, and (physical) efficient causality — each presupposing the last, culminating in the Passion as instrumental physical cause transmitting grace, not merely a moral cause obtaining it. **(3)** Why did Christ suffer so much when the smallest act of love would have sufficed superabundantly? Three reasons: we needed the highest possible testimony of love and heroic example; Christ himself, as priest, could offer no worthier victim than himself, wholly consumed in body, heart, and soul; and the Father willed the greatest victory over sin (by the greatest act of charity), over the devil (by the greatest obedience/humility, opposing pride), and over death (crowned by the Resurrection).

## Mariology

Flows from the same "mother idea" as the Incarnation treatise: the **divine maternity** is the ground of all Mary's graces and privileges, just as the hypostatic union is the ground of Christ's.

- **Predestination**: by the *same* decree as Christ's, and (per Aquinas) likewise dependent, *in genere causae materialis perficiendae*, on the foreseen sin of Adam — if Adam had not sinned, Mary would not have been the Mother of God. Absolutely gratuitous: she could not merit *de condigno* nor *de congruo proprie* to be Mother of God (against Gabriel Biel) — the principle of merit cannot itself fall under merit. "The Blessed Virgin is said to have merited to bear the Lord Jesus Christ, not because she merited that God become incarnate, but because she merited, from grace given to her, that degree of purity and holiness fitting to be Mother of God" (quoted directly) — a *merit of fittingness* (*de congruo*), presupposing the Incarnation rather than earning it.
- **Dignity of divine maternity**: a real relation (from Mary's side) to the hypostatic order, giving her an "infinite dignity" derivative of its term (God) and grounding the cult of **hyperdulia**. A live debate (Contenson, Gotti, Hugon, Merkelbach affirming; Suárez, Vázquez, the Carmelites of Salamanca denying) over whether divine maternity, *considered nakedly*, even outranks sanctifying grace and the beatific vision *simpliciter* — the affirmative view rests on maternity's hypostatic-order term, its role as *reason for* (not consequence of) Mary's grace, and the hyperdulia owed to her specifically as Mother of God rather than as the greatest saint.
- **Holiness — the Immaculate Conception question**: Garrigou-Lagrange, following Mandonnet/Del Prado/Hugon/Vosté, traces a documented **development in Aquinas's own textual record**: an early (1253-54) full affirmation of the privilege → a middle period where, to safeguard Mary's *need* of Christ's redemption (against exempting her from Rom 5:18's universal principle), Aquinas restricts himself to saying sanctification followed ensoulment "*cito post*" (shortly after) without fixing the exact instant, and states she "contracted" original sin (referring, per this reading, to the *debitum culpae* attached to ordinary bodily conception, which for Aquinas *temporally* precedes ensoulment) → late-career texts (1272-73, including the *Expositio Salutationis Angelicae*) apparently returning to unqualified affirmation ("*nec mortale nec veniale peccatum incurrit*"). Garrigou-Lagrange treats this reading (shared by Capponi, John of St. Thomas, Del Prado, Hugon, Vosté — against Suárez, Pesch, Billot, Merkelbach, who read Aquinas as consistently negative) as "seriously probable" but not certain.
- **Fullness of grace**: because divine maternity is of the hypostatic order, Mary's *initial* fullness of grace probably already exceeded the *final* grace of all angels and saints combined — and her charity never ceased increasing until death, following the general principle that souls approaching God accelerate as they near him (unlike a stone thrown upward, which decelerates).
- **Universal mediation**: grounded in (1) co-operation in Calvary's satisfaction/merit — not *de condigno* (strict justice, as Christ's) but *de congruo proprie* ("fittingness," grounded in the "*ius amicabile*" of charity uniting her to God) — earning her the title Coredemptrix; and (2) her continual intercession, obtaining and distributing all graces we receive, possibly (though not certain) even as *physical* instrumental cause paralleling Christ's humanity's instrumental physical causality of grace.
